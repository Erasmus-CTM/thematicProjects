Introduction

So far we have always analysed one variable at a time. In practice, however, we are often interested in the relationships between two variables:

  • Are larger penguins also heavier?
  • Is sex related to body size?
  • Which penguin species live on which islands?

When analysing two variables, three basic questions arise:

Two-way frequency distribution

If both variables are nominal or ordinal (with finitely many values), their joint distribution is summarized in a contingency table (also: cross table).

Definition 1 (Contingency table)

Let X and Y be two variables with values a_1, \dots, a_l (for X) and b_1, \dots, b_k (for Y). The contingency table shows how often each combination (a_{j_1}, b_{j_2}) occurs in the sample. The marginal totals (row/column totals) give the one-dimensional frequency distributions of the individual variables.

Example 1 (Penguins: species and island)

Which penguin species live on which islands?

island     Biscoe  Dream  Torgersen  Total
species                                   
Adelie         44     56         52    152
Chinstrap       0     68          0     68
Gentoo        124      0          0    124
Total         168    124         52    344

The table shows: Gentoo penguins live exclusively on Biscoe, Chinstrap only on Dream. Adelie penguins are found on all three islands. Species and island are therefore strongly related – the variables are not independent.

Independence

Two variables are called independent in the sample if the occurrence of a value of one variable gives no information about the other. You can recognize this by the relative frequency of each cell being equal to the product of the corresponding marginal frequencies:

h_{X,Y}(a_j, b_k) = h_X(a_j) \cdot h_Y(b_k) \quad \text{(for all combinations)}

If this is not the case, there is a relationship.

Cramér’s V – strength of the relationship

To measure the strength of the relationship between two nominal variables, we use Cramér’s V. It always lies between 0 and 1:

  • V = 0: no relationship (independence)
  • V = 1: maximal relationship

Definition 2 (Chi-squared coefficient and Cramér’s V)

Let X and Y be two variables with values a_1, \dots, a_l and b_1, \dots, b_k in a sample of size n, and let every value occur at least once. The chi-squared coefficient measures how far the joint relative frequencies deviate from the values that would be expected under independence:

\chi^2 = n \sum_{i=1}^{l} \sum_{j=1}^{k} \frac{\bigl(h_{X,Y}(a_i, b_j) - h_X(a_i) \cdot h_Y(b_j)\bigr)^2}{h_X(a_i) \cdot h_Y(b_j)}

Cramér’s V normalises this value to the range from 0 to 1:

V = \sqrt{\frac{\chi^2}{n \cdot (\min(l, k) - 1)}}

\chi^2 = 0 (and therefore V = 0) holds exactly when the variables are independent in the sample.

from scipy.stats import chi2_contingency
import numpy as np

# Contingency table
tab = pd.crosstab(df['variable_x'], df['variable_y'])

# Cramér's V
# correction=False: for 2x2 tables scipy otherwise applies a continuity correction
# that does not match the formula from the definition. For larger tables it changes nothing.
chi2, p, dof, expected = chi2_contingency(tab, correction=False)
n = tab.sum().sum()
k = min(tab.shape) - 1
cramers_v = np.sqrt(chi2 / (n * k))

Covariance and correlation

For metric variables there are more precise measures of association that quantify both the direction and the strength of a linear relationship.

Covariance

As a first step towards the correlation coefficient we compute the covariance. It measures whether large x values tend to occur together with large y values (positive relationship) or with small ones (negative relationship).

Definition 3 (Empirical covariance)

s_{x,y} = \dfrac{1}{n-1} \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})

  • s_{x,y} > 0: positive relationship
  • s_{x,y} = 0: no linear relationship
  • s_{x,y} < 0: negative relationship

However, the covariance depends on the scale: if we measure weight in grams instead of kilograms, its value changes – although the relationship has not changed. That is why we normalize it.

Pearson’s correlation coefficient

Definition 4 (Empirical correlation coefficient (Pearson))

r_{x,y} = \dfrac{s_{x,y}}{s_x \cdot s_y} = \dfrac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sqrt{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2} \cdot \sqrt{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar{y})^2}}

It always holds that -1 \le r_{x,y} \le 1.

Important properties:

  • r_{x,y} > 0: positive linear relationship
  • r_{x,y} < 0: negative linear relationship
  • |r_{x,y}| = 1: exact linear relationship (all points on a straight line)
  • r_{x,y} = 0: no linear relationship (but other forms of relationship are possible!)

Spearman’s rank correlation

Spearman’s correlation coefficient is an alternative to Pearson’s coefficient. It is more robust against outliers and also detects non-linear but monotonic relationships. To compute it, the ranks of the observations are determined first, and then Pearson’s coefficient is applied to the ranks.

TipWhich coefficient when?
Pearson Spearman
Measures linear relationships monotonic relationships
Outliers sensitive robust
Scale metric ordinal or metric
Python .corr() .corr(method='spearman')

TipWhat is behind it

Pearson uses the distances to the mean, so a point far away counts heavily. Spearman only uses the ranks: a point at the far bottom right has the largest x rank and the smallest y rank, no matter how far away it is. That is why r_s reacts less strongly to outliers. On a rising curve the ranks of x and y are identical, so r_s = 1 although r < 1. A U shape gives r \approx 0 and r_s \approx 0: both only measure relationships in one direction.

Simple linear regression

Once we have found a linear relationship, we can determine the regression line: the line \hat{g}(x) = \hat{\alpha} + \hat{\beta} \cdot x that describes the data best.

“Best” means: the sum of the squared vertical distances (residuals) between the data points and the line is minimized. This method is called the method of least squares.

Definition 5 (Regression line (least squares))

Let \hat{g}(x) = \hat{\alpha} + \hat{\beta} \cdot x be the line we are looking for. The coefficients are chosen such that \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat{g}(x_i))^2 is minimal.

The least squares estimators are:

\hat{\beta} = \dfrac{s_{x,y}}{s_x^2} = \dfrac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2}

\hat{\alpha} = \bar{y} - \hat{\beta} \cdot \bar{x}

TipInterpreting the parameters
  • \hat{\beta} (slope): if X increases by 1 unit, Y changes by \hat{\beta} units.
  • \hat{\alpha} (intercept): predicted Y value for X = 0. Caution: this is not always meaningful (e.g. weight at a height of 0 cm).

Example 2 (Body height and weight of 12 people)

Known: \bar{x} = 174.63, \bar{y} = 70.75, s_{x,y} = 139.4, s_x^2 = 110.5

\hat{\beta} = \frac{139.4}{110.5} \approx 1.262 \qquad \hat{\alpha} = 70.75 - 1.262 \cdot 174.63 \approx -149.6

\hat{g}(x) = -149.6 + 1.262 \cdot x

Interpretation: for every 1 cm more body height, the weight in this sample increases by about 1.26 kg.

Anscombe’s quartet: always plot too!

Correlation and regression line alone do not tell the whole story. Anscombe’s quartet (1973) shows four data sets with almost identical summary statistics that nevertheless look fundamentally different:

CautionRule: always look at the scatter plot first!

The regression lines and correlation coefficients of the four data sets are practically identical. Only the scatter plot shows:

    1. a normal linear relationship
    1. a quadratic relationship – linear regression is the wrong choice!
    1. a linear relationship with one massive outlier
    1. purely vertical scatter – no real relationship

Correlation ≠ causation

A statistical relationship (correlation) does not mean that one variable causes the other (causation). This is one of the most important insights in statistics.

Example 3 (Babies and storks)

In some European countries a positive correlation between the stork population and the birth rate was observed. Does that mean storks bring babies?

No – the third variable industrialization explains both:

flowchart TB
  A(Industrialization) -->|–| B(Stork population)
  A -->|–| C(Birth rate)

Industrialization reduces both the stork population (through urbanization) and the birth rate. The correlation between storks and births is a spurious correlation.

Example 4 (Relative age effect)

In youth sport, age groups are usually formed by calendar year. Studies show: children born in January are clearly over-represented in selection teams compared to children born in December. At school, too, the youngest children of a class do worse on average. Does the month of birth make you successful?

No – what matters is the age within the group:

flowchart LR
  A(Month of birth) -->|cut-off date| C(Age within the<br>class / age group)
  C --> D(Success at school / in sport)

Someone born shortly after the cut-off date (in sport: in January) is almost a year older than the children in the same group who were born shortly before it. At a young age this age difference matters a lot for development – not the month of birth as such.

TipThree possible explanations for a correlation
Scenario Example
X causes Y smoking → lung cancer (causal)
Y causes X many paramedics on site ← many injured people (reverse causation: the injured are the reason for the paramedics, not the other way round)
Z causes both (spurious correlation) industrialization → storks and birth rate

A correlation alone is no proof of causation. Conversely, a causal relationship can exist without a (linear) correlation, e.g. if it is non-linear or masked by a confounding variable. Causation is established by controlled experiments, not by observational data.

## Zweidimensionale Häufigkeitsverteilung

Sind beide Merkmale **nominal oder ordinal** (mit endlich vielen Ausprägungen), fasst man ihre gemeinsame Verteilung in einer **Kontingenztabelle** (auch: Kreuztabelle) zusammen.

::: {#def-kontingenz}
### Kontingenztabelle

<br>

Seien $X$ und $Y$ zwei Merkmale mit Ausprägungen $a_1, \dots, a_l$ (für $X$) und $b_1, \dots, b_k$ (für $Y$). Die **Kontingenztabelle** zeigt, wie oft jede Kombination $(a_{j_1}, b_{j_2})$ in der Stichprobe vorkommt. Die **Randsummen** (Zeilen-/Spaltensummen) geben die eindimensionalen Häufigkeitsverteilungen der einzelnen Merkmale wieder.
:::

::: {#exm-kontingenz-pinguine}

<br>


:::

### Unabhängigkeit {.unnumbered}

Zwei Merkmale heißen **unabhängig** in der Stichprobe, wenn das Auftreten einer Ausprägung des einen Merkmals keine Information über das andere liefert. Das erkennt man daran, dass die relativen Häufigkeiten jeder Zelle dem Produkt der entsprechenden Randhäufigkeiten entsprechen:

$$h_{X,Y}(a_j, b_k) = h_X(a_j) \cdot h_Y(b_k) \quad \text{(für alle Kombinationen)}$$

Ist das **nicht** der Fall, besteht ein Zusammenhang.

### Cramérs V – Stärke des Zusammenhangs {.unnumbered}

Um die **Stärke** des Zusammenhangs zweier nominaler Merkmale zu messen, nutzt man das **Cramérs V** (auch: Cramérs Kontingenzmaß). Es liegt immer zwischen 0 und 1:

- $V = 0$: kein Zusammenhang (Unabhängigkeit)
- $V = 1$: maximaler Zusammenhang

::: {#def-cramers-v}
### Chi-Quadrat-Koeffizient und Cramérs V

<br>

Seien $X$ und $Y$ zwei Merkmale mit Ausprägungen $a_1, \dots, a_l$ und $b_1, \dots, b_k$ in einer Stichprobe vom Umfang $n$, und jede Ausprägung komme mindestens einmal vor. Der **Chi-Quadrat-Koeffizient** misst, wie weit die gemeinsamen relativen Häufigkeiten von den Werten abweichen, die bei Unabhängigkeit zu erwarten wären:

$$\chi^2 = n \sum_{i=1}^{l} \sum_{j=1}^{k} \frac{\bigl(h_{X,Y}(a_i, b_j) - h_X(a_i) \cdot h_Y(b_j)\bigr)^2}{h_X(a_i) \cdot h_Y(b_j)}$$

**Cramérs V** normiert diesen Wert auf den Bereich von 0 bis 1:

$$V = \sqrt{\frac{\chi^2}{n \cdot (\min(l, k) - 1)}}$$
:::

Es gilt $\chi^2 = 0$ (und damit $V = 0$) genau dann, wenn die Merkmale in der Stichprobe unabhängig sind.

## Kovarianz und Korrelation

Für **kardinal skalierte** Merkmale gibt es präzisere Zusammenhangsmaße, die sowohl **Richtung** als auch **Stärke** eines linearen Zusammenhangs quantifizieren.

### Kovarianz {.unnumbered}

Als Vorstufe zum Korrelationskoeffizienten berechnen wir zunächst die **Kovarianz**. Sie misst, ob große $x$-Werte tendenziell mit großen $y$-Werten auftreten (positiver Zusammenhang) oder mit kleinen (negativer Zusammenhang).

::: {#def-kovarianz}
### Empirische Kovarianz

<br>

$$s_{x,y} = \dfrac{1}{n-1} \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})$$

- $s_{x,y} > 0$: positiver Zusammenhang
- $s_{x,y} = 0$: kein linearer Zusammenhang
- $s_{x,y} < 0$: negativer Zusammenhang
:::

Die Kovarianz ist allerdings **skalenabhängig**: Messen wir Gewicht in Gramm statt Kilogramm, ändert sich ihr Wert – obwohl sich der Zusammenhang nicht geändert hat. Deshalb normieren wir sie.

### Korrelationskoeffizient nach Pearson {.unnumbered}

::: {#def-pearson}
### Empirischer Korrelationskoeffizient (Pearson)

<br>

$$r_{x,y} = \dfrac{s_{x,y}}{s_x \cdot s_y} = \dfrac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sqrt{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2} \cdot \sqrt{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar{y})^2}}$$

Es gilt immer $-1 \le r_{x,y} \le 1$.
:::

```{python}

    (-1.0, 'r = -1 (exakt negativ)'),

    (-0.3, 'r ≈ -0.3 (schwach negativ)'),
    (0.0,  'r ≈ 0 (kein Zusammenhang)'),
    (0.3,  'r ≈ 0.3 (schwach positiv)'),
    (0.8,  'r ≈ 0.8 (stark positiv)'),
    (1.0,  'r = 1 (exakt positiv)'),
]

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 7, figsize=(14, 2.5))
for ax, (rho, label) in zip(axes, configs):
    if abs(rho) == 1.0:
        y = rho * x
    else:
        cov = [[1, rho], [rho, 1]]
        xy = np.random.multivariate_normal([0, 0], cov, 200)
        x_, y = xy[:, 0], xy[:, 1]
        x = x_
    ax.scatter(x, y, s=8, alpha=0.5, color='#005f73')
    ax.set_title(label, fontsize=7.5)
    ax.set_xticks([]); ax.set_yticks([])
```

- $r_{x,y} = 0$: kein **linearer** Zusammenhang (kann aber andere Formen von Zusammenhang geben!)

### Rangkorrelation nach Spearman {.unnumbered}

Der **Spearman-Korrelationskoeffizient** ist eine Alternative zum Pearson-Koeffizienten. Er ist **robuster gegenüber Ausreißern** und erkennt auch **nicht-lineare, aber monotone** Zusammenhänge. Dazu werden zunächst die Ränge der Beobachtungen bestimmt, und dann der Pearson-Koeffizient auf die Ränge angewandt.

::: {.callout-tip}
#### Wann welcher Koeffizient?

| | Pearson | Spearman |
|--|---------|----------|
| **Misst** | lineare Zusammenhänge | monotone Zusammenhänge |
| **Ausreißer** | empfindlich | robust |
| **Skala** | kardinal | ordinal oder kardinal |
| **Python** | `.corr()` | `.corr(method='spearman')` |
:::

Probiere aus:

- Ziehe **einen** Punkt ganz nach rechts unten. Wie stark ändert sich $r$, wie stark $r_s$? Was macht die Gerade?
- Lege die Punkte auf eine Kurve, die immer steigt, aber erst flach und dann steil (wie eine Exponentialfunktion). Welcher Koeffizient erreicht 1?
- Schaffst du $r \approx 0$, obwohl die Punkte einem klaren Muster folgen?

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Was dahintersteckt"}
Pearson verwendet die Abstände zum Mittelwert, ein weit entfernter Punkt zählt deshalb stark. Spearman verwendet nur die **Ränge**: Ein Punkt ganz rechts unten ist beim $x$-Rang der größte und beim $y$-Rang der kleinste, egal wie weit er entfernt ist. Deshalb reagiert $r_s$ auf Ausreißer schwächer. Bei einer steigenden Kurve sind die Ränge von $x$ und $y$ identisch, also $r_s = 1$, obwohl $r < 1$. Eine U-Form ergibt $r \approx 0$ und $r_s \approx 0$: Beide messen nur **gleichgerichtete** Zusammenhänge.
:::

## Einfache lineare Regression

Haben wir einen linearen Zusammenhang festgestellt, können wir die **Regressionsgerade** bestimmen: die Gerade $\hat{g}(x) = \hat{\alpha} + \hat{\beta} \cdot x$, die die Daten am besten beschreibt.

"Am besten" bedeutet: Die **Summe der quadrierten vertikalen Abstände** (Residuen) zwischen den Datenpunkten und der Geraden wird minimiert. Diese Methode heißt **Methode der kleinsten Quadrate**.

::: {#def-regression}
### Regressionsgerade (Kleinste-Quadrate)

<br>

Sei $\hat{g}(x) = \hat{\alpha} + \hat{\beta} \cdot x$ die gesuchte Gerade. Die Koeffizienten werden so gewählt, dass $\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat{g}(x_i))^2$ minimal ist.

Die **Kleinste-Quadrate-Schätzer** lauten:

$$\hat{\beta} = \dfrac{s_{x,y}}{s_x^2} = \dfrac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2}$$

$$\hat{\alpha} = \bar{y} - \hat{\beta} \cdot \bar{x}$$
:::

::: {.callout-tip}
#### Interpretation der Parameter

- $\hat{\beta}$ (**Steigung**): Wenn $X$ um 1 Einheit steigt, verändert sich $Y$ um $\hat{\beta}$ Einheiten.
- $\hat{\alpha}$ (**Achsenabschnitt**): Vorhergesagter $Y$-Wert für $X = 0$. Achtung: Das muss nicht immer inhaltlich sinnvoll sein (z.B. Gewicht bei Größe = 0 cm).
:::

::: {#exm-regression-groesse}
### Körpergröße und -gewicht bei 12 Personen

<br>

Bekannt: $\bar{x} = 174{,}63$, $\bar{y} = 70{,}75$, $s_{x,y} = 139{,}4$, $s_x^2 = 110{,}5$

$$\hat{\beta} = \frac{139{,}4}{110{,}5} \approx 1{,}262 \qquad \hat{\alpha} = 70{,}75 - 1{,}262 \cdot 174{,}63 \approx -149{,}6$$

$$\hat{g}(x) = -149{,}6 + 1{,}262 \cdot x$$

**Interpretation:** Pro 1 cm mehr Körpergröße steigt das Gewicht in dieser Stichprobe um ca. 1,26 kg.
:::

## Korrelation ≠ Kausalität

Ein statistischer Zusammenhang (Korrelation) bedeutet **nicht**, dass ein Merkmal das andere verursacht (Kausalität). Dies ist eine der wichtigsten Erkenntnisse der Statistik.

::: {#exm-storch}
### Babys und Störche

<br>

In manchen europäischen Ländern wurde eine positive Korrelation zwischen der **Storchenpopulation** und der **Geburtenrate** beobachtet. Bedeutet das, dass Störche Babys bringen?

Nein – das **Drittmerkmal Industrialisierung** erklärt beides:

```{mermaid}
%%| eval: true
flowchart TB
  A(Industrialisierung) -->|–| B(Storchenpopulation)
  A -->|–| C(Geburtenrate)
```

Die Industrialisierung reduziert sowohl die Storchenpopulation (durch Verstädterung) als auch die Geburtenrate. Die Korrelation zwischen Störchen und Geburten ist eine **Scheinkorrelation**.
:::

::: {#exm-alterseffekt}
### Relativer Alterseffekt

<br>

Im Jugendsport werden Altersklassen meist nach Kalenderjahr gebildet. Studien zeigen: Im **Januar geborene Kinder** sind in Auswahlmannschaften deutlich häufiger vertreten als im **Dezember geborene**. Auch in der Schule schneiden die jüngsten Kinder einer Klasse im Schnitt schlechter ab. Macht der Geburtsmonat erfolgreich?

Nein – entscheidend ist das **Alter innerhalb der Gruppe**:

```{mermaid}
%%| eval: true
flowchart LR
  A(Geburtsmonat) -->|Stichtag| C(Alter innerhalb der<br>Klasse / Altersklasse)
  C --> D(Schulerfolg / Sporterfolg)
```

Wer kurz **nach** dem Stichtag geboren ist (im Sport: im Januar), ist fast ein Jahr älter als die Kinder derselben Gruppe, die kurz **davor** geboren sind. In jungen Jahren macht dieser Altersunterschied entwicklungsbedingt viel aus – nicht der Geburtsmonat an sich.
:::

::: {.callout-tip}
#### Drei mögliche Erklärungen für eine Korrelation

| Szenario | Beispiel |
|----------|---------|
| **X verursacht Y** | Rauchen → Lungenkrebs (kausal) |
| **Y verursacht X** | Viele Rettungssanitäter vor Ort ← viele Verletzte (Umkehrkausalität: die Verletzten sind der Grund für die Sanitäter, nicht umgekehrt) |
| **Z verursacht beides** (Scheinkorrelation) | Industrialisierung → Störche und Geburtenrate |

Eine Korrelation allein ist **kein Beleg** für Kausalität. Umgekehrt kann ein kausaler Zusammenhang auch ohne (lineare) Korrelation bestehen, etwa wenn er nicht linear ist oder von einer Störvariable überdeckt wird. Kausalität beweist man durch **kontrollierte Experimente**, nicht durch Beobachtungsdaten.
:::

## Two-way frequency distribution {#zweidimensionale-häufigkeitsverteilung}

If both variables are **nominal or ordinal** (with finitely many values), their joint distribution is summarized in a **contingency table** (also: cross table).

::: {#def-kontingenz}
### Contingency table

<br>

Let $X$ and $Y$ be two variables with values $a_1, \dots, a_l$ (for $X$) and $b_1, \dots, b_k$ (for $Y$). The **contingency table** shows how often each combination $(a_{j_1}, b_{j_2})$ occurs in the sample. The **marginal totals** (row/column totals) give the one-dimensional frequency distributions of the individual variables.
:::

::: {#exm-kontingenz-pinguine}
### Penguins: species and island

<br>

Which penguin species live on which islands?

```{python}

```

The table shows: Gentoo penguins live exclusively on Biscoe, Chinstrap only on Dream. Adelie penguins are found on all three islands. Species and island are therefore **strongly related** – the variables are not independent.
:::

### Independence {#unabhängigkeit .unnumbered}

Two variables are called **independent** in the sample if the occurrence of a value of one variable gives no information about the other. You can recognize this by the relative frequency of each cell being equal to the product of the corresponding marginal frequencies:

$$h_{X,Y}(a_j, b_k) = h_X(a_j) \cdot h_Y(b_k) \quad \text{(for all combinations)}$$

If this is **not** the case, there is a relationship.

### Cramér's V – strength of the relationship {#cramérs-v-stärke-des-zusammenhangs .unnumbered}

To measure the **strength** of the relationship between two nominal variables, we use **Cramér's V**. It always lies between 0 and 1:

- $V = 0$: no relationship (independence)
- $V = 1$: maximal relationship

::: {#def-cramers-v}
### Chi-squared coefficient and Cramér's V

<br>

Let $X$ and $Y$ be two variables with values $a_1, \dots, a_l$ and $b_1, \dots, b_k$ in a sample of size $n$, and let every value occur at least once. The **chi-squared coefficient** measures how far the joint relative frequencies deviate from the values that would be expected under independence:

$$\chi^2 = n \sum_{i=1}^{l} \sum_{j=1}^{k} \frac{\bigl(h_{X,Y}(a_i, b_j) - h_X(a_i) \cdot h_Y(b_j)\bigr)^2}{h_X(a_i) \cdot h_Y(b_j)}$$

**Cramér's V** normalises this value to the range from 0 to 1:

$$V = \sqrt{\frac{\chi^2}{n \cdot (\min(l, k) - 1)}}$$
:::

$\chi^2 = 0$ (and therefore $V = 0$) holds exactly when the variables are independent in the sample.

## Covariance and correlation {#kovarianz-und-korrelation}

For **metric** variables there are more precise measures of association that quantify both the **direction** and the **strength** of a linear relationship.

### Covariance {#kovarianz .unnumbered}

As a first step towards the correlation coefficient we compute the **covariance**. It measures whether large $x$ values tend to occur together with large $y$ values (positive relationship) or with small ones (negative relationship).

::: {#def-kovarianz}
### Empirical covariance

<br>

$$s_{x,y} = \dfrac{1}{n-1} \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})$$

- $s_{x,y} > 0$: positive relationship
- $s_{x,y} = 0$: no linear relationship
- $s_{x,y} < 0$: negative relationship
:::

However, the covariance **depends on the scale**: if we measure weight in grams instead of kilograms, its value changes – although the relationship has not changed. That is why we normalize it.

### Pearson's correlation coefficient {#korrelationskoeffizient-nach-pearson .unnumbered}

::: {#def-pearson}
### Empirical correlation coefficient (Pearson)

<br>

$$r_{x,y} = \dfrac{s_{x,y}}{s_x \cdot s_y} = \dfrac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sqrt{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2} \cdot \sqrt{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar{y})^2}}$$

It always holds that $-1 \le r_{x,y} \le 1$.
:::

### Spearman's rank correlation {#rangkorrelation-nach-spearman .unnumbered}

**Spearman's correlation coefficient** is an alternative to Pearson's coefficient. It is **more robust against outliers** and also detects **non-linear but monotonic** relationships. To compute it, the ranks of the observations are determined first, and then Pearson's coefficient is applied to the ranks.

::: {.callout-tip}
#### Which coefficient when?

| | Pearson | Spearman |
|--|---------|----------|
| **Measures** | linear relationships | monotonic relationships |
| **Outliers** | sensitive | robust |
| **Scale** | metric | ordinal or metric |
| **Python** | `.corr()` | `.corr(method='spearman')` |
:::

## Simple linear regression {#einfache-lineare-regression}

Once we have found a linear relationship, we can determine the **regression line**: the line $\hat{g}(x) = \hat{\alpha} + \hat{\beta} \cdot x$ that describes the data best.

"Best" means: the **sum of the squared vertical distances** (residuals) between the data points and the line is minimized. This method is called the **method of least squares**.

::: {#def-regression}
### Regression line (least squares)

<br>

Let $\hat{g}(x) = \hat{\alpha} + \hat{\beta} \cdot x$ be the line we are looking for. The coefficients are chosen such that $\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat{g}(x_i))^2$ is minimal.

The **least squares estimators** are:

$$\hat{\beta} = \dfrac{s_{x,y}}{s_x^2} = \dfrac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2}$$

$$\hat{\alpha} = \bar{y} - \hat{\beta} \cdot \bar{x}$$
:::

::: {.callout-tip}
#### Interpreting the parameters

- $\hat{\beta}$ (**slope**): if $X$ increases by 1 unit, $Y$ changes by $\hat{\beta}$ units.
- $\hat{\alpha}$ (**intercept**): predicted $Y$ value for $X = 0$. Caution: this is not always meaningful (e.g. weight at a height of 0 cm).
:::

::: {#exm-regression-groesse}
### Body height and weight of 12 people

<br>

Known: $\bar{x} = 174.63$, $\bar{y} = 70.75$, $s_{x,y} = 139.4$, $s_x^2 = 110.5$

$$\hat{\beta} = \frac{139.4}{110.5} \approx 1.262 \qquad \hat{\alpha} = 70.75 - 1.262 \cdot 174.63 \approx -149.6$$

$$\hat{g}(x) = -149.6 + 1.262 \cdot x$$

**Interpretation:** for every 1 cm more body height, the weight in this sample increases by about 1.26 kg.
:::

```{python}
    x_line = np.array([0, 20])
    ax.plot(x_line, slope * x_line + intercept, color='#ae2012', lw=1.5)
    ax.set_title(f'Data set {num}\n$r = {r:.3f}$', fontsize=10)
    ax.set_xlim(0, 20); ax.set_ylim(2, 14)
    ax.set_xlabel('x'); ax.set_ylabel('y') if num == 1 else None
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()
```

::: {.callout-caution}
#### Rule: always look at the scatter plot first!

The regression lines and correlation coefficients of the four data sets are practically identical. Only the scatter plot shows:

- (1) a normal linear relationship
- (2) a quadratic relationship – linear regression is the wrong choice!
- (3) a linear relationship with one massive outlier
- (4) purely vertical scatter – no real relationship

**Summary statistics do not replace graphical data analysis!**
:::

## Correlation ≠ causation {#korrelation-kausalität}

A statistical relationship (correlation) does **not** mean that one variable causes the other (causation). This is one of the most important insights in statistics.

::: {#exm-storch}
### Babies and storks

<br>

In some European countries a positive correlation between the **stork population** and the **birth rate** was observed. Does that mean storks bring babies?

No – the **third variable industrialization** explains both:

```{mermaid}
%%| eval: true
flowchart TB
  A(Industrialization) -->|–| B(Stork population)
  A -->|–| C(Birth rate)
```

Industrialization reduces both the stork population (through urbanization) and the birth rate. The correlation between storks and births is a **spurious correlation**.
:::

::: {#exm-alterseffekt}
### Relative age effect

<br>

In youth sport, age groups are usually formed by calendar year. Studies show: children born in **January** are clearly over-represented in selection teams compared to children born in **December**. At school, too, the youngest children of a class do worse on average. Does the month of birth make you successful?

No – what matters is the **age within the group**:

```{mermaid}
%%| eval: true
flowchart LR
  A(Month of birth) -->|cut-off date| C(Age within the<br>class / age group)
  C --> D(Success at school / in sport)
```

Someone born shortly **after** the cut-off date (in sport: in January) is almost a year older than the children in the same group who were born shortly **before** it. At a young age this age difference matters a lot for development – not the month of birth as such.
:::

::: {.callout-tip}
#### Three possible explanations for a correlation

| Scenario | Example |
|----------|---------|
| **X causes Y** | smoking → lung cancer (causal) |
| **Y causes X** | many paramedics on site ← many injured people (reverse causation: the injured are the reason for the paramedics, not the other way round) |
| **Z causes both** (spurious correlation) | industrialization → storks and birth rate |

A correlation alone is **no proof** of causation. Conversely, a causal relationship can exist without a (linear) correlation, e.g. if it is non-linear or masked by a confounding variable. Causation is established by **controlled experiments**, not by observational data.
:::

::: {#exr-korrelation-kausalitaet}

## Summary {#zusammenfassung}

::: {.callout-tip}
#### The key points at a glance

**Graphical analysis:**

- Scatter plot for two metric variables
- Grouped box plots / histograms for nominal + metric
- Contingency table for two nominal variables

**Measures of association:**

| Measure | Types of variables | Range | Python |
|-----|--------------|---------|--------|
| **Cramér's V** | nominal × nominal | [0, 1] | `chi2_contingency` + formula |
| **Pearson** $r$ | metric × metric | [–1, 1] | `.corr()` |
| **Spearman** $r_s$ | ordinal/metric | [–1, 1] | `.corr(method='spearman')` |

**Regression:**

- Regression line $\hat{g}(x) = \hat{\alpha} + \hat{\beta} \cdot x$ with the least squares method
- $\hat{\beta} = s_{x,y} / s_x^2$, $\hat{\alpha} = \bar{y} - \hat{\beta}\bar{x}$

**Most important warning:** correlation ≠ causation. **Always look at the scatter plot!**
