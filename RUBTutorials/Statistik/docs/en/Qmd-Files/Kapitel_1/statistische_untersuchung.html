1.1 A Statistical Study

Descriptive Statistics

Your answers are saved only in this browser.

Learning objectives: By the end of this chapter you can

  • explain the basic steps of a statistical analysis.
  • explain the terms observational unit, population, sample, variable and values of a variable and give your own examples.
  • explain the differences between the types of variables and assign the right type to a variable.
  • group quantitative variables into classes.

Introduction

Statistics helps us to gain information from data. That sounds abstract – but you actually do it every day:

  • You read that “60% of respondents are in favor of measure X” and ask yourself: Is that really everyone? Or just 100 people?
  • You see that your grade average has dropped and wonder: in which subject was that?
  • A study claims that coffee drinkers live longer. You ask: Did they really examine that, or could it be due to something else?

That is exactly what statistics is – systematic, with methods that we get to know in this tutorial.

The steps of a statistical study

A typical statistical analysis proceeds in six steps:

  • Steps
  • Example
  1. Formulate a question or hypothesis
  2. Collect or gather data
  3. Prepare the data (clean, merge, handle missing values)
  4. Descriptive analysis – describe and visualize the data
  5. Model-based analysis – test hypotheses, infer to the population
  6. Report or presentation of the results

These steps can be repeated if necessary.

Topic: Are basketball players taller on average than handball players?

  1. Hypothesis: Basketball players are taller on average than handball players.

  2. Data: Body heights from sports databases (e.g. Bundesliga squads).

  3. Preparation: If the data come from different sources, they have to be merged. Are all heights in cm? How do we deal with missing values?

  4. Descriptive analysis: We compute the average, minimum and maximum for each sport and create graphics. Result in this sample: basketball players ∅ 1.98 m, handball players ∅ 1.95 m.

    Caution

    Watch out – confounders! If, for example, 10% of the basketball sample are women but 40% of the handball sample, then we see a height difference that has nothing to do with the sport. That is why you should always separate by sex!

  5. Model-based analysis: Hypothesis tests check whether the difference could have arisen by chance or is statistically significant (→ section 2.5).

  6. Report: Explain the methodology, interpret the results, name the limitations (e.g.: we only looked at professional sport).

Basic terms

For a statistical study we first need three central terms:

Definition 1 (Observational unit, population and sample)  

  • A single object or individual is called an observational unit (also: statistical unit).
  • The set of all observational units is called the population.
  • A sample is a subset of the population that was actually examined. Its size is called the sample size n.

Why is a sample enough? Because a census – surveying all observational units – is usually too expensive, too time-consuming or simply impossible. Instead of catching all the penguins of the Palmer Archipelago, you examine 344.

Example 1 (Our dataset: Palmer penguins)  

  • Research question: How heavy are penguins on the Palmer Archipelago?
  • Observational unit: a single penguin on the Palmer Archipelago
  • Population: all penguins on the Palmer Archipelago
  • Sample: the 344 recorded penguins from the years 2007–2009
  • Criteria for delimitation:
    • factual: penguin species (Adélie, Chinstrap, Gentoo), sex
    • spatial: island (Biscoe, Dream, Torgersen)
    • temporal: year of recording (2007, 2008, 2009)
Tip

Random sample: For results from the sample to be transferable to the population, every observational unit should have the same chance of getting into the sample. This is called a random sample.

Exercise 1 (Your own research question) Think of a statistical question from your everyday life (e.g. about school, sport, music). Name:

  • the observational unit,
  • the population,
  • a possible sample,
  • one factual, one spatial and one temporal criterion each.

Variables and types of variables

We are not interested in the observational units themselves, but in their properties – the so-called variables.

Definition 2 (Variable and value of a variable)  

  • A variable is a property of the observational units that is recorded (e.g. body weight, penguin species, sex).
  • The possible values of a variable are called its values or categories (e.g. 3750 g, “Adélie”, “female”).
  • Variables are written with capital letters (X, Y, …), observed values with lowercase letters (x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n).

Variables are classified by their kind – because different types require different statistical methods:

Definition 3 (Levels of measurement) Qualitative (categorical) variables describe properties without arithmetic operations:

  • Nominal: categories without a natural order. Examples: penguin species, island, blood type, sex
  • Ordinal: categories with a meaningful order, but without a measurable distance. Examples: school grades, satisfaction (low/medium/high), Nutri-Score

Quantitative (metric) variables are numerical values that you can calculate with:

  • Cardinal (also: metric): values can be ordered and their distances compared. Examples: body height, weight, temperature, age, bill length

Cardinal variables can be subdivided further:

  • Interval scale: distances can be interpreted, but there is no natural zero point. Example: temperature in °C (0 °C does not mean “no temperature”).
  • Ratio scale: there is a natural zero point; ratios are meaningful. Example: weight (6000 g is twice as heavy as 3000 g).

In addition, one distinguishes:

  • Discrete variables: finitely many or countably infinitely many values (e.g. number of eggs, school grade).
  • Continuous variables: uncountably many possible values, e.g. every real number in an interval (e.g. body height, weight).
Tip

Rule of thumb: Can I calculate meaningfully (take an average, interpret differences)? → cardinal. Is there a natural order but no measurable distances? → ordinal. Otherwise → nominal.

Grouping quantitative variables into classes

Sometimes a quantitative variable has a great many different values. To get a better overview, you combine values into classes – you group (bin) the variable.

Definition 4 (Class boundaries, class width and class midpoint) If you divide a range of values into l classes with boundaries k_0 < k_1 < \cdots < k_l, a value a belongs to the j-th class if a \in (k_{j-1},\, k_j].

  • Class boundaries: k_{j-1} (lower) and k_j (upper boundary)
  • Class width: k_j - k_{j-1}
  • Class midpoint: \tilde{a}_j = \dfrac{k_{j-1} + k_j}{2}

Example 3 (Exam grades) An exam has 60 points. The grades are created by grouping into classes (German grades, 1 is best):

Grade Class Class width Class midpoint
5 [0, 20] 20 10
4 (20, 30] 10 25
3 (30, 40] 10 35
2 (40, 50] 10 45
1 (50, 60] 10 55

The grouped variable (grade) is ordinal – you can order it, but distances between grades cannot be interpreted meaningfully.

Rule of thumb: A grouping should result in between 5 and 20 classes. The classes must be disjoint (no overlap) and cover the entire range of values.

Summary

  • Statistics gains information from data – systematically, in six steps.
  • The population contains all objects of interest; we usually only examine a sample.
  • Variables describe properties of the observational units. Depending on the type of variable (nominal, ordinal, cardinal), different methods are available.
  • Quantitative variables can be grouped into classes to gain an overview – this turns a cardinal variable into an ordinal one.

## Einführung

Statistik hilft uns, aus Daten Informationen zu gewinnen. Das klingt abstrakt – aber du machst das eigentlich jeden Tag:

- Du liest, dass *„60 % der Befragten für Maßnahme X sind"* und fragst dich: Sind das wirklich alle? Oder nur 100 Leute?
- Du siehst, dass deine Durchschnittsnote gesunken ist und überlegst: In welchem Fach war das?
- Eine Studie behauptet, Kaffeetrinker leben länger. Du fragst: Haben die das wirklich untersucht, oder liegt es vielleicht an etwas anderem?

Genau das ist Statistik – systematisch, mit Methoden, die wir in diesem Tutorial kennenlernen.

## Die Schritte einer statistischen Untersuchung

Eine typische statistische Analyse läuft in sechs Schritten ab:

::: {.panel-tabset}

### Schritte

1. **Fragestellung oder Hypothese** formulieren
2. **Daten erheben oder sammeln**
3. **Daten aufbereiten** (bereinigen, zusammenführen, fehlende Werte behandeln)
4. **Deskriptive Analyse** – Daten beschreiben und visualisieren
5. **Modellbasierte Analyse** – Hypothesen testen, auf Grundgesamtheit schließen
6. **Bericht oder Präsentation** der Ergebnisse

Diese Schritte können bei Bedarf wiederholt werden.

### Beispiel

**Thema:** Sind Basketballspieler im Schnitt größer als Handballspieler?

1. **Hypothese:** Basketballspieler sind im Mittel größer als Handballspieler.

2. **Daten:** Körpergrößen aus Sportdatenbanken (z.B. Bundesliga-Kader).

3. **Aufbereitung:** Kommen die Daten aus verschiedenen Quellen, müssen sie zusammengeführt werden. Sind alle Größen in cm? Wie gehen wir mit fehlenden Werten um?

4. **Deskriptive Analyse:** Wir berechnen Durchschnitt, Minimum und Maximum für jede Sportart und erstellen Grafiken. Ergebnis in dieser Stichprobe: Basketballspieler ∅ 1,98 m, Handballspieler ∅ 1,95 m.

   ::: {.callout-caution}
   **Achtung – Störvariable!** Wenn z.B. in der Basketball-Stichprobe 10 % Frauen sind, in der Handball-Stichprobe aber 40 %, dann sehen wir einen Größenunterschied, der nichts mit der Sportart zu tun hat. Deshalb sollte man immer nach Geschlecht trennen!
   :::

5. **Modellbasierte Analyse:** Hypothesentests prüfen, ob der Unterschied zufällig entstanden sein könnte oder *statistisch signifikant* ist (→ Abschnitt [2.5](../Kapitel_3/signifikanz_p_werte.qmd)).

6. **Bericht:** Methodik erläutern, Ergebnisse interpretieren, Limitationen benennen (z.B.: Wir haben nur Profisport betrachtet).

:::

## Grundbegriffe

Für eine statistische Untersuchung brauchen wir zunächst drei zentrale Begriffe:

::: {#def-grundbegriffe}
### Untersuchungseinheit, Grundgesamtheit und Stichprobe

- Ein einzelnes Objekt oder Individuum heißt **Untersuchungseinheit** (auch: Merkmalsträger).
- Die Menge *aller* Untersuchungseinheiten heißt **Grundgesamtheit** (oder Population).
- Eine **Stichprobe** ist eine Teilmenge der Grundgesamtheit, die tatsächlich untersucht wurde. Ihre Größe heißt **Stichprobenumfang** $n$.
:::

Warum reicht eine Stichprobe? Weil eine **Vollerhebung** – also alle Untersuchungseinheiten zu befragen – meist zu teuer, zu zeitaufwändig oder schlicht unmöglich ist. Statt alle Pinguine des Palmer-Archipels zu fangen, untersucht man 344.

::: {#exm-pinguine-grundbegriffe}
### Unser Datensatz: Palmer-Pinguine

- **Untersuchungsfrage:** Wie schwer sind Pinguine auf dem Palmer-Archipel?
- **Untersuchungseinheit:** ein einzelner Pinguin auf dem Palmer-Archipel
- **Grundgesamtheit:** alle Pinguine auf dem Palmer-Archipel
- **Stichprobe:** die 344 erfassten Pinguine aus den Jahren 2007–2009
- **Kriterien** zur Abgrenzung:
  - *sachlich:* Pinguinart (Adélie, Chinstrap, Gentoo), Geschlecht
  - *räumlich:* Insel (Biscoe, Dream, Torgersen)
  - *zeitlich:* Erfassungsjahr (2007, 2008, 2009)
:::

::: {.callout-tip}
**Zufallsstichprobe:** Damit Ergebnisse aus der Stichprobe auf die Grundgesamtheit übertragbar sind, sollte jede Untersuchungseinheit die gleiche Chance haben, in die Stichprobe zu gelangen. Man nennt das eine *Zufallsstichprobe*.
:::

::: {#exr-grundbegriffe}
### Eigene Fragestellung

Überlege dir eine statistische Fragestellung aus deinem Alltag (z.B. über Schule, Sport, Musik). Benenne dazu:

- die Untersuchungseinheit,
- die Grundgesamtheit,
- eine mögliche Stichprobe,
- je ein sachliches, räumliches und zeitliches Kriterium.
:::

## Merkmale und Merkmalstypen

Uns interessieren nicht die Untersuchungseinheiten an sich, sondern ihre **Eigenschaften** – die sogenannten Merkmale.

::: {#def-merkmale}
### Merkmal und Merkmalsausprägung

- Ein **Merkmal** ist eine Eigenschaft der Untersuchungseinheiten, die erfasst wird (z.B. Körpergewicht, Pinguinart, Geschlecht).
- Die möglichen Werte eines Merkmals heißen **Merkmalsausprägungen** (z.B. 3750 g, „Adélie", „weiblich").
- Merkmale werden mit Großbuchstaben notiert ($X$, $Y$, ...), beobachtete Werte mit Kleinbuchstaben ($x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n$).
:::

Merkmale werden nach ihrer **Art** eingeteilt – weil unterschiedliche Typen unterschiedliche statistische Methoden erfordern:

::: {#def-skalenniveaus}
### Skalenniveaus

**Qualitative (kategoriale) Merkmale** bezeichnen Eigenschaften ohne Rechenoperationen:

- **Nominal:** Kategorien ohne natürliche Reihenfolge.
  *Beispiele:* Pinguinart, Insel, Blutgruppe, Geschlecht
- **Ordinal:** Kategorien mit sinnvoller Reihenfolge, aber ohne messbaren Abstand.
  *Beispiele:* Schulnoten, Zufriedenheit (gering/mittel/hoch), Nutri-Score

**Quantitative (metrische) Merkmale** sind Zahlenwerte, mit denen gerechnet werden kann:

- **Kardinal** (auch: metrisch): Ausprägungen können geordnet und ihre Abstände verglichen werden.
  *Beispiele:* Körpergröße, Gewicht, Temperatur, Alter, Schnabellänge
:::

Kardinale Merkmale lassen sich nochmals unterteilen:

- **Intervallskala:** Abstände sind interpretierbar, aber es gibt keinen natürlichen Nullpunkt. *Beispiel:* Temperatur in °C (0 °C bedeutet nicht „keine Temperatur").
- **Verhältnisskala:** Es gibt einen natürlichen Nullpunkt; Verhältnisse sind sinnvoll. *Beispiel:* Gewicht (6000 g ist doppelt so schwer wie 3000 g).

Außerdem unterscheidet man:

- **Diskrete Merkmale:** endlich viele oder abzählbar unendlich viele Ausprägungen (z.B. Anzahl Eier, Schulnote).
- **Stetige Merkmale:** überabzählbar viele mögliche Werte, z.B. jede reelle Zahl in einem Intervall (z.B. Körpergröße, Gewicht).

::: {.callout-tip}
**Faustregel:** Kann ich sinnvoll rechnen (Durchschnitt bilden, Differenzen interpretieren)? → kardinal. Gibt es eine natürliche Reihenfolge, aber keine messbaren Abstände? → ordinal. Sonst → nominal.
:::

## Klassieren quantitativer Merkmale

Manchmal hat ein quantitatives Merkmal sehr viele verschiedene Ausprägungen. Um einen besseren Überblick zu bekommen, fasst man Werte zu **Klassen** zusammen – man *klassiert* das Merkmal.

::: {#def-klassierung}
### Klassengrenzen, Klassenbreite und Klassenmitte

Teilt man einen Wertebereich in $l$ Klassen ein mit Grenzen $k_0 < k_1 < \cdots < k_l$, so gehört ein Wert $a$ zur $j$-ten Klasse, wenn $a \in (k_{j-1},\, k_j]$.

- **Klassengrenzen:** $k_{j-1}$ (untere) und $k_j$ (obere Grenze)
- **Klassenbreite:** $k_j - k_{j-1}$
- **Klassenmitte:** $\tilde{a}_j = \dfrac{k_{j-1} + k_j}{2}$
:::

::: {#exm-klassierung-noten}
### Klausurnoten

Eine Klausur hat 60 Punkte. Die Noten entstehen durch Klassierung:

| Note | Klasse | Klassenbreite | Klassenmitte |
|------|--------|---------------|--------------|
| 5 | [0, 20] | 20 | 10 |
| 4 | (20, 30] | 10 | 25 |
| 3 | (30, 40] | 10 | 35 |
| 2 | (40, 50] | 10 | 45 |
| 1 | (50, 60] | 10 | 55 |

Das klassierte Merkmal (Note) ist **ordinal** skaliert – man kann ordnen, aber Abstände zwischen Noten sind nicht sinnvoll interpretierbar.
:::

::: {.callout-rechnen}
**Faustregel:** Eine Klassierung sollte zwischen 5 und 20 Klassen ergeben. Die Klassen müssen *disjunkt* sein (keine Überlappung) und den gesamten Wertebereich abdecken.
:::

Probiere die Definition einmal aus. Im Code unten ist die Gewichtsklasse „mittel“ das Intervall $(3500,\, 5500]$ (in Gramm):


Jetzt klassieren wir das Körpergewicht der Pinguine in Python:

::: {.callout-python}
**Python-Tipp:** `pd.cut()` klassiert eine Spalte direkt. Mit `labels=` gibst du den Klassen Namen.
:::

```{pyodide-python}
#| read-only: true
import pandas as pd
from pyodide.http import open_url

penguins = pd.read_csv(open_url("https://raw.githubusercontent.com/allisonhorst/palmerpenguins/main/inst/extdata/penguins.csv"))

# Gewicht in drei Klassen einteilen
penguins["gewichtsklasse"] = pd.cut(
    penguins["body_mass_g"],
    bins=[2500, 3500, 5500, 6500],
    labels=["leicht", "mittel", "schwer"]
)

# Wie viele Pinguine in jeder Klasse?
print(penguins["gewichtsklasse"].value_counts().sort_index())
```

## Introduction {#einführung}

Statistics helps us to gain information from data. That sounds abstract – but you actually do it every day:

- You read that *"60% of respondents are in favor of measure X"* and ask yourself: Is that really everyone? Or just 100 people?
- You see that your grade average has dropped and wonder: in which subject was that?
- A study claims that coffee drinkers live longer. You ask: Did they really examine that, or could it be due to something else?

That is exactly what statistics is – systematic, with methods that we get to know in this tutorial.

## The steps of a statistical study {#die-schritte-einer-statistischen-untersuchung}

A typical statistical analysis proceeds in six steps:

::: {.panel-tabset}

### Steps

1. Formulate a **question or hypothesis**
2. **Collect or gather data**
3. **Prepare the data** (clean, merge, handle missing values)
4. **Descriptive analysis** – describe and visualize the data
5. **Model-based analysis** – test hypotheses, infer to the population
6. **Report or presentation** of the results

These steps can be repeated if necessary.

### Example

**Topic:** Are basketball players taller on average than handball players?

1. **Hypothesis:** Basketball players are taller on average than handball players.

2. **Data:** Body heights from sports databases (e.g. Bundesliga squads).

3. **Preparation:** If the data come from different sources, they have to be merged. Are all heights in cm? How do we deal with missing values?

4. **Descriptive analysis:** We compute the average, minimum and maximum for each sport and create graphics. Result in this sample: basketball players ∅ 1.98 m, handball players ∅ 1.95 m.

   ::: {.callout-caution}
   **Watch out – confounders!** If, for example, 10% of the basketball sample are women but 40% of the handball sample, then we see a height difference that has nothing to do with the sport. That is why you should always separate by sex!
   :::

5. **Model-based analysis:** Hypothesis tests check whether the difference could have arisen by chance or is *statistically significant* (→ section [2.5](../Kapitel_3/signifikanz_p_werte.qmd)).

6. **Report:** Explain the methodology, interpret the results, name the limitations (e.g.: we only looked at professional sport).

:::

## Basic terms {#grundbegriffe}

For a statistical study we first need three central terms:

::: {#def-grundbegriffe}
### Observational unit, population and sample

- A single object or individual is called an **observational unit** (also: statistical unit).
- The set of *all* observational units is called the **population**.
- A **sample** is a subset of the population that was actually examined. Its size is called the **sample size** $n$.
:::

Why is a sample enough? Because a **census** – surveying all observational units – is usually too expensive, too time-consuming or simply impossible. Instead of catching all the penguins of the Palmer Archipelago, you examine 344.

::: {#exm-pinguine-grundbegriffe}
### Our dataset: Palmer penguins

- **Research question:** How heavy are penguins on the Palmer Archipelago?
- **Observational unit:** a single penguin on the Palmer Archipelago
- **Population:** all penguins on the Palmer Archipelago
- **Sample:** the 344 recorded penguins from the years 2007–2009
- **Criteria** for delimitation:
  - *factual:* penguin species (Adélie, Chinstrap, Gentoo), sex
  - *spatial:* island (Biscoe, Dream, Torgersen)
  - *temporal:* year of recording (2007, 2008, 2009)
:::

::: {.callout-tip}
**Random sample:** For results from the sample to be transferable to the population, every observational unit should have the same chance of getting into the sample. This is called a *random sample*.
:::

## Variables and types of variables {#merkmale-und-merkmalstypen}

We are not interested in the observational units themselves, but in their **properties** – the so-called variables.

::: {#def-merkmale}
### Variable and value of a variable

- A **variable** is a property of the observational units that is recorded (e.g. body weight, penguin species, sex).
- The possible values of a variable are called its **values** or categories (e.g. 3750 g, "Adélie", "female").
- Variables are written with capital letters ($X$, $Y$, ...), observed values with lowercase letters ($x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n$).
:::

Variables are classified by their **kind** – because different types require different statistical methods:

::: {#def-skalenniveaus}
### Levels of measurement

**Qualitative (categorical) variables** describe properties without arithmetic operations:

- **Nominal:** categories without a natural order.
  *Examples:* penguin species, island, blood type, sex
- **Ordinal:** categories with a meaningful order, but without a measurable distance.
  *Examples:* school grades, satisfaction (low/medium/high), Nutri-Score

**Quantitative (metric) variables** are numerical values that you can calculate with:

- **Cardinal** (also: metric): values can be ordered and their distances compared.
  *Examples:* body height, weight, temperature, age, bill length
:::

Cardinal variables can be subdivided further:

- **Interval scale:** distances can be interpreted, but there is no natural zero point. *Example:* temperature in °C (0 °C does not mean "no temperature").
- **Ratio scale:** there is a natural zero point; ratios are meaningful. *Example:* weight (6000 g is twice as heavy as 3000 g).

In addition, one distinguishes:

- **Discrete variables:** finitely many or countably infinitely many values (e.g. number of eggs, school grade).
- **Continuous variables:** uncountably many possible values, e.g. every real number in an interval (e.g. body height, weight).

::: {.callout-tip}
**Rule of thumb:** Can I calculate meaningfully (take an average, interpret differences)? → cardinal. Is there a natural order but no measurable distances? → ordinal. Otherwise → nominal.
:::

## Grouping quantitative variables into classes {#klassieren-quantitativer-merkmale}

Sometimes a quantitative variable has a great many different values. To get a better overview, you combine values into **classes** – you *group* (bin) the variable.

::: {#def-klassierung}
### Class boundaries, class width and class midpoint

If you divide a range of values into $l$ classes with boundaries $k_0 < k_1 < \cdots < k_l$, a value $a$ belongs to the $j$-th class if $a \in (k_{j-1},\, k_j]$.

- **Class boundaries:** $k_{j-1}$ (lower) and $k_j$ (upper boundary)
- **Class width:** $k_j - k_{j-1}$
- **Class midpoint:** $\tilde{a}_j = \dfrac{k_{j-1} + k_j}{2}$
:::

::: {#exm-klassierung-noten}
### Exam grades

An exam has 60 points. The grades are created by grouping into classes (German grades, 1 is best):

| Grade | Class | Class width | Class midpoint |
|------|--------|---------------|--------------|
| 5 | [0, 20] | 20 | 10 |
| 4 | (20, 30] | 10 | 25 |
| 3 | (30, 40] | 10 | 35 |
| 2 | (40, 50] | 10 | 45 |
| 1 | (50, 60] | 10 | 55 |

The grouped variable (grade) is **ordinal** – you can order it, but distances between grades cannot be interpreted meaningfully.
:::

::: {.callout-rechnen}
**Rule of thumb:** A grouping should result in between 5 and 20 classes. The classes must be *disjoint* (no overlap) and cover the entire range of values.
:::

Try the definition. In the code below, the weight class "medium" is the interval $(3500,\, 5500]$ (in grams):

```{math-exercise}

#| caption: Class width and class midpoint
```

Now we group the body weight of the penguins into classes in Python:

::: {.callout-python}
**Python tip:** `pd.cut()` groups a column directly. With `labels=` you give the classes names.
:::

```{pyodide-python}
#| read-only: true
import pandas as pd
from pyodide.http import open_url

penguins = pd.read_csv(open_url("https://raw.githubusercontent.com/allisonhorst/palmerpenguins/main/inst/extdata/penguins.csv"))

# Divide weight into three classes
penguins["weight_class"] = pd.cut(
    penguins["body_mass_g"],
    bins=[2500, 3500, 5500, 6500],
    labels=["light", "medium", "heavy"]
)

# How many penguins are in each class?
print(penguins["weight_class"].value_counts().sort_index())
```

## Summary {#zusammenfassung}

- Statistics gains **information from data** – systematically, in six steps.
- The **population** contains all objects of interest; we usually only examine a **sample**.
- **Variables** describe properties of the observational units. Depending on the **type of variable** (nominal, ordinal, cardinal), different methods are available.
- Quantitative variables can be **grouped into classes** to gain an overview – this turns a cardinal variable into an ordinal one.
