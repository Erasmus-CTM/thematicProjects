2.4 Confidence Intervals

Your answers are saved only in this browser.

Learning objectives

By the end of this section you can:

  • explain what a confidence interval is – and what it is not.
  • compute a confidence interval for the expected value with known variance (z-interval).
  • compute a confidence interval for the expected value with unknown variance (t-interval).
  • describe how the sample size n and the confidence level 1-\alpha affect the width of the interval.

The problem with point estimates

An estimate on its own tells us little. If we compute that the mean body mass of the Adélie penguins in our dataset is \bar{x} = 3700.7 g, we still do not know how far this value is from the true mean \mu of all Adélie penguins on the Palmer Archipelago.

What we need: an interval around the estimate that shows how precise it is. It should come from a procedure that captures the true parameter in the vast majority of cases.

What is a confidence interval?

NoteDefinition: confidence interval

A (1-\alpha) confidence interval (CI) for the parameter \theta is a random interval [\hat{\theta}_l, \hat{\theta}_u] that covers the true parameter with probability 1-\alpha: P(\hat{\theta}_l \leq \theta \leq \hat{\theta}_u) = 1-\alpha

The quantity 1-\alpha is called the confidence level (e.g. 0.95 = 95 %). The quantity \alpha is called the error probability.

The correct interpretation

The confidence interval is often misinterpreted. Here is the crucial difference:

CautionCommon misconception

Wrong: “With 95 % probability the true parameter \mu lies in the interval [a, b].”

Why it is wrong: \mu is a fixed quantity, not a random variable. Either \mu lies in [a, b] or it does not – once the interval has been computed, nothing random is left.

Right: “The procedure that produces this interval yields, for 95 % of all possible samples, an interval that contains the true parameter.”

Each of the 40 samples gives a different confidence interval. About 95 % of them (teal lines) contain the true parameter – a few (red lines) miss it.

CI for the expected value: variance known (z-interval)

Derivation

Suppose the data X_1, \ldots, X_n are normally distributed with known variance \sigma^2. Then the sample mean is normally distributed as well: \overline{X}_n \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mu, \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right)

Standardizing gives a quantity with a known standard normal distribution: Z = \frac{\overline{X}_n - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)

For a symmetric interval around 0, with the (1-\alpha/2) quantile z_{1-\alpha/2}: P\!\left(-z_{1-\alpha/2} \leq Z \leq z_{1-\alpha/2}\right) = 1-\alpha

Substituting and solving for \mu gives the confidence interval:

Notez confidence interval for \mu (variance \sigma^2 known)

\left[\overline{X}_n - z_{1-\alpha/2} \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}},\; \overline{X}_n + z_{1-\alpha/2} \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\right]

Important quantiles: z_{0.975} = 1.960 (for a 95% CI) and z_{0.995} = 2.576 (for a 99% CI).

Example: bill length of the penguins

We estimate the mean bill length of all Adélie penguins. We assume that \sigma = 2.5 mm is known.

Compute the standard error, the half-width \Delta and the 95% CI (to two decimal places). How many animals do you need at least so that the half-width is at most 0.5 mm?

z confidence interval by hand
\(\text{SE} =\) mm, \(\Delta =\) mm, 95% CI: \([\) \(,\) \(]\) mm, \(n \geq\)
TipSolution steps
  • \text{SE} = \sigma/\sqrt{n} = 2.5/5 = 0.5 mm
  • \Delta = z_{0.975} \cdot \text{SE} = 1.96 \cdot 0.5 = 0.98 mm
  • CI: 38.8 \pm 0.98 = [37.82,\ 39.78] mm

CI for the expected value: variance unknown (t-interval)

The problem

In practice we usually do not know \sigma. We have to replace \sigma by s (the sample standard deviation). But then the standardized quantity is no longer standard normally distributed: T = \frac{\overline{X}_n - \mu}{s/\sqrt{n}} \sim t_{n-1}

It follows a t-distribution with n-1 degrees of freedom. This accounts for the additional uncertainty from estimating \sigma.

The t-distribution

The t-distribution has heavier tails than the normal distribution – the CI becomes somewhat wider. The more degrees of freedom (= n-1), the closer the t-distribution is to the normal distribution. From n \geq 30 on, the difference is usually small.

Notet confidence interval for \mu (variance \sigma^2 unknown)

\left[\overline{X}_n - t_{n-1;\,1-\alpha/2} \cdot \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}},\; \overline{X}_n + t_{n-1;\,1-\alpha/2} \cdot \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}\right]

Here t_{n-1;\,1-\alpha/2} is the (1-\alpha/2) quantile of the t-distribution with n-1 degrees of freedom.

TipWhen z, when t?
Situation Method
\sigma known, normal distribution z-interval
\sigma unknown, normal distribution t-interval
\sigma unknown, large n (\geq 30) t-interval (≈ z-interval)
Small sample, strong departure from normality Nonparametric methods (not covered here)

In practice: The t-interval is used almost always, since \sigma is rarely known.

Example: body mass of all penguins

Python tip: mass.sem() directly computes the estimated standard error \hat{\text{SE}} = s/\sqrt{n}.

What affects the width of the interval

The width of a confidence interval depends on three factors:

\text{Width} = 2 \cdot t_{n-1;\,1-\alpha/2} \cdot \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}

Factor Effect on CI width
Larger n Narrower CI (factor 1/\sqrt{n})
Higher confidence level 1-\alpha Wider CI (larger quantile)
Larger spread \sigma Wider CI (directly proportional)

TipWhat is behind it

At 95 %, on average 47.5 of 50 intervals are hits. Sometimes it is 50, sometimes 45 – that is chance. A larger n makes all intervals narrower but does not change the number of hits: the means move closer to \mu at the same rate (\sqrt{n}) at which the intervals shrink. The hit rate depends only on the level. A higher level makes every interval wider, and in return fewer of them miss. More certainty therefore costs precision, and only more data helps with both.

Practical example: confidence intervals by penguin species

The interval of the Gentoo penguins lies far to the right and overlaps with neither of the others: Gentoos are clearly heavier on average. CautionCareful with the reverse conclusion

If two confidence intervals do not overlap, that is strong evidence of a real difference. If they overlap slightly, the difference can still be significant. For the difference of two means you need an interval of its own or a test.

Summary

Method Assumption Formula
z-interval \sigma known, normal distribution \bar{x} \pm z_{1-\alpha/2} \cdot \sigma/\sqrt{n}
t-interval \sigma unknown, normal distribution \bar{x} \pm t_{n-1;\,1-\alpha/2} \cdot s/\sqrt{n}

Correct interpretation: A 95% confidence interval means that the procedure yields, for 95 % of all possible samples, an interval that contains the true parameter. For a concrete, realized interval [a, b] no probability statement can be made any more.

## Das Problem der Punktschätzung

Ein Schätzwert allein sagt uns wenig. Wenn wir berechnen, dass das mittlere Körpergewicht der
Adélie-Pinguine in unserem Datensatz $\bar{x} = 3700{,}7$ g beträgt, wissen wir noch nicht, wie
weit dieser Wert vom **wahren** Mittelwert $\mu$ aller Adélie-Pinguine auf dem Palmer-Archipel entfernt ist.

Was wir brauchen: ein **Intervall** um den Schätzwert, das zeigt, wie genau er ist. Es soll von einem
Verfahren stammen, das den wahren Parameter in den allermeisten Fällen einfängt.

---

## Was ist ein Konfidenzintervall?

::: {.callout-note icon=false}
## Definition: Konfidenzintervall

Ein **$(1-\alpha)$-Konfidenzintervall** (KI) für den Parameter $\theta$ ist ein zufälliges
Intervall $[\hat{\theta}_u, \hat{\theta}_o]$, das den wahren Parameter mit Wahrscheinlichkeit
$1-\alpha$ überdeckt:
$$P(\hat{\theta}_u \leq \theta \leq \hat{\theta}_o) = 1-\alpha$$

Die Größe $1-\alpha$ heißt **Konfidenzniveau** (z.B. 0,95 = 95 %).
Die Größe $\alpha$ heißt **Irrtumswahrscheinlichkeit**.
:::

### Die richtige Interpretation

Das Konfidenzintervall wird oft falsch interpretiert. Hier ist der entscheidende Unterschied:

::: {.callout-caution}
## Häufiges Missverständnis

**Falsch:** "Mit 95 % Wahrscheinlichkeit liegt der wahre Parameter $\mu$ im Intervall $[a, b]$."

**Warum falsch:** $\mu$ ist eine feste Größe, keine Zufallsvariable. Entweder liegt $\mu$ in $[a, b]$
oder nicht – aber nach der Berechnung ist das kein Zufallsvorgang mehr.

**Richtig:** "Das Verfahren, das dieses Intervall erzeugt, liefert bei 95 % aller möglichen
Stichproben ein Intervall, das den wahren Parameter enthält."
:::

```{python}
#| echo: false
#| eval: true
np.random.seed(42)
true_mu = 3700.0
true_sigma = 460.0
n = 30
alpha = 0.05
m = 40  # Anzahl simulierter Stichproben

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(10, 7))

hits = 0
for i in range(m):
    sample = np.random.normal(true_mu, true_sigma, n)
    x_bar = sample.mean()
    se = true_sigma / np.sqrt(n)
    z = stats.norm.ppf(1 - alpha/2)
    lo, hi = x_bar - z*se, x_bar + z*se
    contains = lo <= true_mu <= hi
    if contains:
        hits += 1
    color = '#0a9396' if contains else '#ae2012'
    ax.hlines(i, lo, hi, color=color, lw=2, alpha=0.8)
    ax.plot(x_bar, i, 'o', color=color, ms=5)

ax.axvline(true_mu, color='k', lw=2.5, ls='--', label=f'Wahrer Wert μ = {true_mu:.0f} g', zorder=5)
ax.set_xlabel('Körpermasse (g)', fontsize=11)
ax.set_ylabel('Stichprobe Nr.')
ax.set_title(f'95%-Konfidenzintervalle für μ: {hits}/{m} = {hits/m*100:.0f}% enthalten μ\n'
             f'(n = {n} je Stichprobe, σ bekannt)', fontsize=11)
from matplotlib.lines import Line2D
legend_elements = [
    Line2D([0],[0], color='#0a9396', lw=2, label='KI enthält μ'),
    Line2D([0],[0], color='#ae2012', lw=2, label='KI verfehlt μ'),
    Line2D([0],[0], color='k', lw=2, ls='--', label='Wahrer Wert μ'),
]
ax.legend(handles=legend_elements, fontsize=9)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

Jede der 40 Stichproben ergibt ein anderes Konfidenzintervall. Etwa 95 % davon (türkise Linien)
enthalten den wahren Parameter – ein paar (rote Linien) verfehlen ihn.

## KI für den Erwartungswert: Varianz bekannt (z-Intervall)

### Herleitung

Nehmen wir an, die Daten $X_1, \ldots, X_n$ sind normalverteilt mit bekannter Varianz $\sigma^2$.
Das Stichprobenmittel ist dann ebenfalls normalverteilt:
$$\overline{X}_n \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mu, \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right)$$

Durch **Standardisierung** erhalten wir eine Größe mit bekannter Standardnormalverteilung:
$$Z = \frac{\overline{X}_n - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$$

Für ein symmetrisches Intervall um 0 gilt mit dem $(1-\alpha/2)$-Quantil $z_{1-\alpha/2}$:
$$P\!\left(-z_{1-\alpha/2} \leq Z \leq z_{1-\alpha/2}\right) = 1-\alpha$$

Einsetzen und Umformen nach $\mu$ ergibt das Konfidenzintervall:

::: {.callout-note icon=false}
## z-Konfidenzintervall für $\mu$ (Varianz $\sigma^2$ bekannt)

$$\left[\overline{X}_n - z_{1-\alpha/2} \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}},\;
         \overline{X}_n + z_{1-\alpha/2} \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\right]$$

Wichtige Quantile: $z_{0{,}975} = 1{,}960$ (für 95%-KI) und $z_{0{,}995} = 2{,}576$ (für 99%-KI).
:::

### Beispiel: Schnabellänge der Pinguine

Wir schätzen die mittlere Schnabellänge aller Adélie-Pinguine. Wir nehmen an, dass $\sigma = 2{,}5$ mm bekannt ist.

```{pyodide-python}
import pandas as pd
from scipy import stats
import numpy as np
from pyodide.http import open_url

penguins = pd.read_csv(open_url("https://raw.githubusercontent.com/allisonhorst/palmerpenguins/main/inst/extdata/penguins.csv"))
bill = penguins[penguins['species'] == 'Adelie']['bill_length_mm'].dropna()

n = len(bill)
x_bar = bill.mean()
sigma = 2.5       # angenommene bekannte Standardabweichung
alpha = 0.05      # Irrtumswahrscheinlichkeit für 95%-KI

z = stats.norm.ppf(1 - alpha/2)   # z_{0.975} = 1.96
margin = z * sigma / np.sqrt(n)

lo, hi = x_bar - margin, x_bar + margin

print(f"n = {n},  x̄ = {x_bar:.2f} mm")
print(f"z = {z:.3f}")
print(f"Halbbreite Δ = {margin:.2f} mm")
print(f"95%-KI: [{lo:.2f}, {hi:.2f}] mm")
```

Eine Stichprobe von $n = 25$ Adélie-Pinguinen hat die mittlere Schnabellänge $\bar{x} = 38{,}8$ mm, die Standardabweichung $\sigma = 2{,}5$ mm sei bekannt. Berechne Standardfehler, Halbbreite $\Delta$ und das 95%-KI (auf zwei Nachkommastellen). Wie viele Tiere braucht man mindestens, damit die Halbbreite höchstens $0{,}5$ mm beträgt?

## KI für den Erwartungswert: Varianz unbekannt (t-Intervall)

### Das Problem

In der Praxis kennen wir $\sigma$ meistens **nicht**. Wir müssen $\sigma$ durch $s$ (die
Stichprobenstandardabweichung) ersetzen. Aber dann ist die standardisierte Größe **nicht mehr**
standardnormalverteilt:
$$T = \frac{\overline{X}_n - \mu}{s/\sqrt{n}} \sim t_{n-1}$$

Sie folgt einer **t-Verteilung mit $n-1$ Freiheitsgraden**. Das berücksichtigt die zusätzliche
Unsicherheit durch das Schätzen von $\sigma$.

### Die t-Verteilung

```{python}
#| echo: false
#| eval: true
x = np.linspace(-5, 5, 500)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 4))
for df, col, lab in [(3,'#ca6702',f't(3)'), (10,'#0a9396',f't(10)'),
                      (30,'#005f73','t(30)'), (np.inf,'#ae2012','N(0,1)')]:
    if df == np.inf:
        y = stats.norm.pdf(x)
    else:
        y = stats.t.pdf(x, df)
    ax.plot(x, y, color=col, lw=2, label=lab)

ax.set_xlabel('t'); ax.set_ylabel('Dichte')
ax.set_title('t-Verteilungen im Vergleich zur Standardnormalverteilung')
ax.legend(); ax.set_xlim(-5, 5)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

Die t-Verteilung hat **schwerere Ränder** als die Normalverteilung – das KI wird etwas breiter.
Je mehr Freiheitsgrade ($= n-1$), desto näher liegt die t-Verteilung an der Normalverteilung.
Ab $n \geq 30$ ist der Unterschied meist klein.

::: {.callout-note icon=false}
## t-Konfidenzintervall für $\mu$ (Varianz $\sigma^2$ unbekannt)

$$\left[\overline{X}_n - t_{n-1;\,1-\alpha/2} \cdot \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}},\;
         \overline{X}_n + t_{n-1;\,1-\alpha/2} \cdot \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}\right]$$

Dabei ist $t_{n-1;\,1-\alpha/2}$ das $(1-\alpha/2)$-Quantil der t-Verteilung mit $n-1$
Freiheitsgraden.
:::

::: {.callout-tip}
## Wann z, wann t?

| Situation | Verfahren |
|-----------|-----------|
| $\sigma$ bekannt, Normalverteilung | z-Intervall |
| $\sigma$ unbekannt, Normalverteilung | t-Intervall |
| $\sigma$ unbekannt, großes $n$ ($\geq 30$) | t-Intervall (≈ z-Intervall) |
| Kleine Stichprobe, starke Abweichung von Normalverteilung | Nichtparametrische Methoden (hier nicht behandelt) |

**In der Praxis:** Fast immer wird das t-Intervall verwendet, da $\sigma$ selten bekannt ist.
:::

## Einflüsse auf die Intervallbreite

Die Breite eines Konfidenzintervalls hängt von drei Faktoren ab:

$$\text{Breite} = 2 \cdot t_{n-1;\,1-\alpha/2} \cdot \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}$$

```{python}
#| echo: false
#| eval: true
np.random.seed(99)
true_mu = 3700.0
true_sigma = 460.0

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(12, 4.5))

# 1. Stichprobengröße
ax = axes[0]
ns = [10, 20, 30, 50, 100, 200]
widths = []
for n in ns:
    t = stats.t.ppf(0.975, df=n-1)
    w = 2 * t * true_sigma / np.sqrt(n)
    widths.append(w)
ax.plot(ns, widths, 'o-', color='#0a9396', lw=2)
ax.set_xlabel('Stichprobenumfang n')
ax.set_ylabel('KI-Breite (g)')
ax.set_title('Einfluss von n\n(α = 0,05, σ = 460 g fest)')
ax.grid(True, alpha=0.3)

# 2. Konfidenzniveau
ax2 = axes[1]
alphas = [0.20, 0.10, 0.05, 0.02, 0.01]
conf_levels = [1 - a for a in alphas]
widths2 = [2 * stats.norm.ppf(1 - a/2) * true_sigma / np.sqrt(50) for a in alphas]
ax2.plot([cl*100 for cl in conf_levels], widths2, 'o-', color='#ae2012', lw=2)
ax2.set_xlabel('Konfidenzniveau (1−α) in %')
ax2.set_ylabel('KI-Breite (g)')
ax2.set_title('Einfluss des Konfidenzniveaus\n(n = 50, σ = 460 g fest)')

# 3. Standardabweichung (Datenvariabilität)
ax3 = axes[2]
sigmas = [100, 200, 300, 400, 500, 600]
widths3 = [2 * stats.norm.ppf(0.975) * s / np.sqrt(50) for s in sigmas]
ax3.plot(sigmas, widths3, 'o-', color='#ca6702', lw=2)
ax3.set_xlabel('Standardabweichung σ (g)')
ax3.set_ylabel('KI-Breite (g)')

ax3.grid(True, alpha=0.3)

plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

| Einfluss | Wirkung auf KI-Breite |
|----------|----------------------|
| Größeres $n$ | Schmaleres KI (Faktor $1/\sqrt{n}$) |
| Höheres Konfidenzniveau $1-\alpha$ | Breiteres KI (größeres Quantil) |
| Größere Streuung $\sigma$ | Breiteres KI (direkt proportional) |

## Zusammenfassung

| Methode | Voraussetzung | Formel |
|---------|---------------|--------|
| **z-Intervall** | $\sigma$ bekannt, Normalverteilung | $\bar{x} \pm z_{1-\alpha/2} \cdot \sigma/\sqrt{n}$ |
| **t-Intervall** | $\sigma$ unbekannt, Normalverteilung | $\bar{x} \pm t_{n-1;\,1-\alpha/2} \cdot s/\sqrt{n}$ |

**Korrekte Interpretation:** Ein 95%-Konfidenzintervall bedeutet, dass das Verfahren bei 95 % aller
möglichen Stichproben ein Intervall liefert, das den wahren Parameter enthält. Für ein konkretes
realisiertes Intervall $[a, b]$ kann man keine Wahrscheinlichkeitsaussage mehr machen.

## The problem with point estimates {#das-problem-der-punktschätzung}

An estimate on its own tells us little. If we compute that the mean body mass of the Adélie
penguins in our dataset is $\bar{x} = 3700.7$ g, we still do not know how far this value is from
the **true** mean $\mu$ of all Adélie penguins on the Palmer Archipelago.

What we need: an **interval** around the estimate that shows how precise it is. It should come
from a procedure that captures the true parameter in the vast majority of cases.

---

## What is a confidence interval? {#was-ist-ein-konfidenzintervall}

::: {.callout-note icon=false}
## Definition: confidence interval

A **$(1-\alpha)$ confidence interval** (CI) for the parameter $\theta$ is a random interval
$[\hat{\theta}_l, \hat{\theta}_u]$ that covers the true parameter with probability $1-\alpha$:
$$P(\hat{\theta}_l \leq \theta \leq \hat{\theta}_u) = 1-\alpha$$

The quantity $1-\alpha$ is called the **confidence level** (e.g. 0.95 = 95 %).
The quantity $\alpha$ is called the **error probability**.
:::

### The correct interpretation {#die-richtige-interpretation}

The confidence interval is often misinterpreted. Here is the crucial difference:

::: {.callout-caution}
## Common misconception

**Wrong:** "With 95 % probability the true parameter $\mu$ lies in the interval $[a, b]$."

**Why it is wrong:** $\mu$ is a fixed quantity, not a random variable. Either $\mu$ lies in
$[a, b]$ or it does not – once the interval has been computed, nothing random is left.

**Right:** "The procedure that produces this interval yields, for 95 % of all possible samples,
an interval that contains the true parameter."
:::

```{python}
#| echo: false
#| eval: true
np.random.seed(42)
true_mu = 3700.0
true_sigma = 460.0
n = 30
alpha = 0.05
m = 40  # number of simulated samples

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(10, 7))

hits = 0
for i in range(m):
    sample = np.random.normal(true_mu, true_sigma, n)
    x_bar = sample.mean()
    se = true_sigma / np.sqrt(n)
    z = stats.norm.ppf(1 - alpha/2)
    lo, hi = x_bar - z*se, x_bar + z*se
    contains = lo <= true_mu <= hi
    if contains:
        hits += 1
    color = '#0a9396' if contains else '#ae2012'
    ax.hlines(i, lo, hi, color=color, lw=2, alpha=0.8)
    ax.plot(x_bar, i, 'o', color=color, ms=5)

ax.set_xlabel('Body mass (g)', fontsize=11)
ax.set_ylabel('Sample no.')
ax.set_title(f'95% confidence intervals for μ: {hits}/{m} = {hits/m*100:.0f}% contain μ\n'
             f'(n = {n} per sample, σ known)', fontsize=11)
from matplotlib.lines import Line2D
legend_elements = [
    Line2D([0],[0], color='#0a9396', lw=2, label='CI contains μ'),
    Line2D([0],[0], color='#ae2012', lw=2, label='CI misses μ'),
    Line2D([0],[0], color='k', lw=2, ls='--', label='True value μ'),
]
ax.legend(handles=legend_elements, fontsize=9)

plt.show()
```

## CI for the expected value: variance known (z-interval) {#ki-für-den-erwartungswert-varianz-bekannt-z-intervall}

### Derivation {#herleitung}

Suppose the data $X_1, \ldots, X_n$ are normally distributed with known variance $\sigma^2$.
Then the sample mean is normally distributed as well:
$$\overline{X}_n \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mu, \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right)$$

**Standardizing** gives a quantity with a known standard normal distribution:
$$Z = \frac{\overline{X}_n - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$$

For a symmetric interval around 0, with the $(1-\alpha/2)$ quantile $z_{1-\alpha/2}$:
$$P\!\left(-z_{1-\alpha/2} \leq Z \leq z_{1-\alpha/2}\right) = 1-\alpha$$

Substituting and solving for $\mu$ gives the confidence interval:

::: {.callout-note icon=false}
## z confidence interval for $\mu$ (variance $\sigma^2$ known)

$$\left[\overline{X}_n - z_{1-\alpha/2} \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}},\;
         \overline{X}_n + z_{1-\alpha/2} \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\right]$$

Important quantiles: $z_{0.975} = 1.960$ (for a 95% CI) and $z_{0.995} = 2.576$ (for a 99% CI).
:::

### Example: bill length of the penguins {#beispiel-schnabellänge-der-pinguine}

We estimate the mean bill length of all Adélie penguins. We assume that $\sigma = 2.5$ mm is known.

```{pyodide-python}
import pandas as pd
from scipy import stats
import numpy as np
from pyodide.http import open_url

penguins = pd.read_csv(open_url("https://raw.githubusercontent.com/allisonhorst/palmerpenguins/main/inst/extdata/penguins.csv"))
bill = penguins[penguins['species'] == 'Adelie']['bill_length_mm'].dropna()

n = len(bill)
x_bar = bill.mean()
sigma = 2.5       # assumed known standard deviation
alpha = 0.05      # error probability for a 95% CI

z = stats.norm.ppf(1 - alpha/2)   # z_{0.975} = 1.96
margin = z * sigma / np.sqrt(n)

lo, hi = x_bar - margin, x_bar + margin

print(f"n = {n},  x̄ = {x_bar:.2f} mm")
print(f"z = {z:.3f}")
print(f"Half-width Δ = {margin:.2f} mm")
print(f"95% CI: [{lo:.2f}, {hi:.2f}] mm")
```

### By hand {#von-hand}

A sample of $n = 25$ Adélie penguins has a mean bill length of $\bar{x} = 38.8$ mm, and the standard deviation $\sigma = 2.5$ mm is assumed known. Compute the standard error, the half-width $\Delta$ and the 95% CI (to two decimal places). How many animals do you need at least so that the half-width is at most $0.5$ mm?

```{math-exercise}
#| label: ki-hand-en
#| caption: z confidence interval by hand
#| mode: numeric
#| tolerance: 0.011
#| partial-credit: true
#| field-labels: SE, Δ, lower bound, upper bound, n
$\text{SE} =$ _[0.5] mm, $\Delta =$ _[0.98] mm, 95% CI: $[$ _[37.82] $,$ _[39.78] $]$ mm, $n \geq$ _[97]
```

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Solution steps"}
- $\text{SE} = \sigma/\sqrt{n} = 2.5/5 = 0.5$ mm
- $\Delta = z_{0.975} \cdot \text{SE} = 1.96 \cdot 0.5 = 0.98$ mm
- CI: $38.8 \pm 0.98 = [37.82,\ 39.78]$ mm
- $1.96 \cdot 2.5/\sqrt{n} \leq 0.5 \iff \sqrt{n} \geq 9.8 \iff n \geq 96.04$, so at least $97$ animals (round up, otherwise the condition is narrowly missed)
:::

## CI for the expected value: variance unknown (t-interval) {#ki-für-den-erwartungswert-varianz-unbekannt-t-intervall}

### The problem {#das-problem}

In practice we usually do **not** know $\sigma$. We have to replace $\sigma$ by $s$ (the sample
standard deviation). But then the standardized quantity is **no longer** standard normally
distributed:
$$T = \frac{\overline{X}_n - \mu}{s/\sqrt{n}} \sim t_{n-1}$$

It follows a **t-distribution with $n-1$ degrees of freedom**. This accounts for the additional
uncertainty from estimating $\sigma$.

### The t-distribution {#die-t-verteilung}

```{python}
#| echo: false
#| eval: true
x = np.linspace(-5, 5, 500)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 4))
for df, col, lab in [(3,'#ca6702',f't(3)'), (10,'#0a9396',f't(10)'),
                      (30,'#005f73','t(30)'), (np.inf,'#ae2012','N(0,1)')]:
    if df == np.inf:
        y = stats.norm.pdf(x)
    else:
        y = stats.t.pdf(x, df)
    ax.plot(x, y, color=col, lw=2, label=lab)

ax.set_xlabel('t'); ax.set_ylabel('Density')
ax.set_title('t-distributions compared with the standard normal distribution')
ax.legend(); ax.set_xlim(-5, 5)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

The t-distribution has **heavier tails** than the normal distribution – the CI becomes somewhat
wider. The more degrees of freedom ($= n-1$), the closer the t-distribution is to the normal
distribution. From $n \geq 30$ on, the difference is usually small.

::: {.callout-note icon=false}
## t confidence interval for $\mu$ (variance $\sigma^2$ unknown)

$$\left[\overline{X}_n - t_{n-1;\,1-\alpha/2} \cdot \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}},\;
         \overline{X}_n + t_{n-1;\,1-\alpha/2} \cdot \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}\right]$$

Here $t_{n-1;\,1-\alpha/2}$ is the $(1-\alpha/2)$ quantile of the t-distribution with $n-1$
degrees of freedom.
:::

::: {.callout-tip}
## When z, when t?

| Situation | Method |
|-----------|--------|
| $\sigma$ known, normal distribution | z-interval |
| $\sigma$ unknown, normal distribution | t-interval |
| $\sigma$ unknown, large $n$ ($\geq 30$) | t-interval (≈ z-interval) |
| Small sample, strong departure from normality | Nonparametric methods (not covered here) |

**In practice:** The t-interval is used almost always, since $\sigma$ is rarely known.
:::

### Example: body mass of all penguins {#beispiel-körpermasse-aller-pinguine}

## What affects the width of the interval {#einflüsse-auf-die-intervallbreite}

The width of a confidence interval depends on three factors:

$$\text{Width} = 2 \cdot t_{n-1;\,1-\alpha/2} \cdot \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}$$

```{python}
#| echo: false
#| eval: true
np.random.seed(99)
true_mu = 3700.0
true_sigma = 460.0

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(12, 4.5))

# 1. Sample size
ax = axes[0]
ns = [10, 20, 30, 50, 100, 200]
widths = []
for n in ns:
    t = stats.t.ppf(0.975, df=n-1)
    w = 2 * t * true_sigma / np.sqrt(n)
    widths.append(w)
ax.plot(ns, widths, 'o-', color='#0a9396', lw=2)
ax.set_xlabel('Sample size n')
ax.set_ylabel('CI width (g)')
ax.set_title('Effect of n\n(α = 0.05, σ = 460 g fixed)')
ax.grid(True, alpha=0.3)

# 2. Confidence level
ax2 = axes[1]
alphas = [0.20, 0.10, 0.05, 0.02, 0.01]
conf_levels = [1 - a for a in alphas]
widths2 = [2 * stats.norm.ppf(1 - a/2) * true_sigma / np.sqrt(50) for a in alphas]
ax2.plot([cl*100 for cl in conf_levels], widths2, 'o-', color='#ae2012', lw=2)
ax2.set_xlabel('Confidence level (1−α) in %')
ax2.set_ylabel('CI width (g)')
ax2.set_title('Effect of the confidence level\n(n = 50, σ = 460 g fixed)')
ax2.grid(True, alpha=0.3)

# 3. Standard deviation (variability of the data)
ax3 = axes[2]
sigmas = [100, 200, 300, 400, 500, 600]
widths3 = [2 * stats.norm.ppf(0.975) * s / np.sqrt(50) for s in sigmas]
ax3.plot(sigmas, widths3, 'o-', color='#ca6702', lw=2)
ax3.set_xlabel('Standard deviation σ (g)')
ax3.set_ylabel('CI width (g)')
ax3.set_title('Effect of data variability\n(n = 50, α = 0.05 fixed)')
ax3.grid(True, alpha=0.3)

plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

| Factor | Effect on CI width |
|----------|----------------------|
| Larger $n$ | Narrower CI (factor $1/\sqrt{n}$) |
| Higher confidence level $1-\alpha$ | Wider CI (larger quantile) |
| Larger spread $\sigma$ | Wider CI (directly proportional) |

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="What is behind it"}
At 95 %, on average $47.5$ of 50 intervals are hits. Sometimes it is 50, sometimes 45 – that is chance. A larger $n$ makes all intervals narrower but does **not** change the number of hits: the means move closer to $\mu$ at the same rate ($\sqrt{n}$) at which the intervals shrink. The hit rate depends only on the level. A higher level makes every interval wider, and in return fewer of them miss. More certainty therefore costs precision, and only more data helps with both.
:::

## Practical example: confidence intervals by penguin species {#praxisbeispiel-konfidenzintervalle-nach-pinguinart}

```{pyodide-python}
import pandas as pd
from scipy import stats
import numpy as np
from pyodide.http import open_url

penguins = pd.read_csv(open_url("https://raw.githubusercontent.com/allisonhorst/palmerpenguins/main/inst/extdata/penguins.csv"))

print(f"{'Species':<12} {'n':>5} {'x̄ (g)':>10} {'SE':>8} {'95% CI':>25}")
print("-" * 65)

for species in ['Adelie', 'Chinstrap', 'Gentoo']:
    mass = penguins[penguins['species'] == species]['body_mass_g'].dropna()
    n = len(mass)
    x_bar = mass.mean()
    se = mass.sem()   # s / sqrt(n)
    lo, hi = stats.t.interval(0.95, df=n-1, loc=x_bar, scale=se)
    print(f"{species:<12} {n:>5} {x_bar:>10.1f} {se:>8.1f}  [{lo:>7.1f}, {hi:>7.1f}]")
```

## Careful with the reverse conclusion

If two confidence intervals do **not** overlap, that is strong evidence of a real difference.
If they overlap **slightly**, the difference can still be significant. For the difference of
two means you need an interval of its own or a test.
:::

## Summary {#zusammenfassung}

| Method | Assumption | Formula |
|---------|---------------|--------|
| **z-interval** | $\sigma$ known, normal distribution | $\bar{x} \pm z_{1-\alpha/2} \cdot \sigma/\sqrt{n}$ |
| **t-interval** | $\sigma$ unknown, normal distribution | $\bar{x} \pm t_{n-1;\,1-\alpha/2} \cdot s/\sqrt{n}$ |

**Correct interpretation:** A 95% confidence interval means that the procedure yields, for 95 % of
all possible samples, an interval that contains the true parameter. For a concrete, realized
interval $[a, b]$ no probability statement can be made any more.
