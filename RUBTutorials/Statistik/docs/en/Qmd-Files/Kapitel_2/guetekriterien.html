2.3 Quality Criteria for Point Estimators

Your answers are saved only in this browser.

Learning objectives

By the end of this section you can:

  • explain the three most important quality criteria for estimators (unbiasedness, efficiency, consistency) and interpret them visually.
  • compute the bias of an estimator and explain why s^2 with 1/(n-1) is unbiased.
  • interpret and compute the standard error of the sample mean \sigma/\sqrt{n}.
  • recognize consistency from simulations.

Introduction: what makes a good estimator?

As we saw in section 2.1, an estimator gives a different estimate for every new sample. When is an estimator good?

A vivid picture: imagine an archer shooting at a target. The center of the target is the true parameter \theta. Every arrow is an estimate from a random sample.

We want an estimator that:

  1. hits the true value on average (unbiasedness),
  2. spreads little (efficiency),
  3. becomes ever more precise with more data (consistency).

Unbiasedness (bias)

NoteDefinition: bias and unbiasedness

The bias of an estimator \hat{\theta} for the parameter \theta is the systematic estimation error: \text{Bias}(\hat{\theta}) = E(\hat{\theta}) - \theta

An estimator is called unbiased if its bias is zero: E(\hat{\theta}) = \theta

The sample mean is unbiased

For independent, identically distributed values with expected value \mu: E(\overline{X}_n) = E\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i\right) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n E(X_i) = \mu

The sample mean is an unbiased estimator for \mu.

Why s^2 needs the factor 1/(n-1)

If you compute the variance with 1/n: \tilde{s}^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2

then E(\tilde{s}^2) = \frac{n-1}{n} \sigma^2 < \sigma^2 – the estimator systematically underestimates the true variance \sigma^2. With the factor 1/(n-1), on the other hand: E(s^2) = \sigma^2 \quad \text{(unbiased!)}

CautionCommon mistake: numpy vs. pandas variance

In Python, numpy and pandas compute the variance differently:

import numpy as np, pandas as pd

x = [2, 4, 6, 8]
np.var(x)       # 1/n → NOT unbiased (= 5.0)
np.var(x, ddof=1)  # 1/(n-1) → unbiased (= 6.667)
pd.Series(x).var()  # 1/(n-1) → unbiased (= 6.667)

To estimate the population variance, always use ddof=1!

Simulation: making the bias visible

For small samples (n = 5), \tilde{s}^2 clearly underestimates the variance. The difference becomes smaller as n grows.

Efficiency and standard error

An estimator is efficient if it spreads little. The central measure for this is the standard error.

NoteDefinition: standard error

The standard error of an estimator \hat{\theta} is its standard deviation: \text{SE}(\hat{\theta}) = \sqrt{\text{Var}(\hat{\theta})}

It measures how much the estimates vary between different samples.

Standard error of the sample mean

For independent, identically distributed values with variance \sigma^2: \text{SE}(\overline{X}_n) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}

Derivation: \text{Var}(\overline{X}_n) = \text{Var}\!\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i\right) = \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^n \text{Var}(X_i) = \frac{n \cdot \sigma^2}{n^2} = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}

Conclusion: With k times as much data, the standard error does not become k times smaller, but only smaller by the factor \sqrt{k}. To double the precision you need four times as much data!

Estimating the standard error in Python

Since \sigma is unknown in practice, the standard error is estimated by: \widehat{\text{SE}}(\overline{X}_n) = \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}

By hand

Body mass varies with \sigma = 460 g. How large is the standard error of \overline{X}_n for n = 25 animals? How many animals do you need for it to be only half as large? And: for \sigma^2 = 4 and n = 5, what expected value and what bias does \tilde{s}^2 have?

Standard error and bias by hand
\(\text{SE} =\) g, \(n =\) , \(E(\tilde{s}^2) =\) , \(\text{Bias}(\tilde{s}^2) =\)
TipSolution steps
  • \text{SE} = 460/\sqrt{25} = 460/5 = 92 g
  • Half the standard error means \sqrt{n} twice as large, so n four times as large: 4 \cdot 25 = 100
  • E(\tilde{s}^2) = \frac{n-1}{n}\sigma^2 = \frac{4}{5} \cdot 4 = 3.2, so \text{Bias} = 3.2 - 4 = -0.8

Consistency

NoteDefinition: consistency

A sequence of estimators (\hat{\theta}_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}} is called consistent if the estimates converge to the true parameter value as the sample size n grows:

\hat{\theta}_n \xrightarrow{n \to \infty} \theta \quad \text{(in probability)}

Intuitively: with enough data the estimate becomes arbitrarily precise.

The sample mean is consistent, because \text{SE}(\overline{X}_n) = \sigma/\sqrt{n} \to 0 for n \to \infty. This is a consequence of the law of large numbers.

Simulation: convergence of the sample mean

The sample mean fluctuates strongly at first, but then gets ever closer to the true value. The ±2·SE band shows how the uncertainty decreases with 1/\sqrt{n}.

MSE: bias and variance together

The mean squared error (MSE) combines bias and variance in one quantity:

\text{MSE}(\hat{\theta}) = \text{Var}(\hat{\theta}) + \text{Bias}(\hat{\theta})^2

This is called the bias-variance decomposition. A good estimator has both small bias and small variance – both terms contribute to the total error.

Surprisingly, \tilde{s}^2 is biased but spreads less than s^2. For normally distributed data its MSE is smaller for every n (dashed line on the right). So unbiasedness is not automatically the most important thing. s^2 is still usually used, because it is right on average and because the formulas for confidence intervals and tests (sections 2.4 and 2.5) build on it.

Summary

Criterion What is assessed? Formula / condition
Unbiasedness Systematic error E(\hat{\theta}) = \theta (Bias = 0)
Standard error Spread of the estimates \text{SE} = \sqrt{\text{Var}(\hat{\theta})}
Consistency Behavior for n \to \infty \hat{\theta}_n \to \theta
MSE Total error \text{MSE} = \text{Var} + \text{Bias}^2

In practice: the standard estimators \overline{X}_n (for \mu) and s^2 (for \sigma^2) are unbiased and consistent. The standard error of the mean is \sigma/\sqrt{n}.

## Einführung: Was macht einen guten Schätzer aus?

Wie wir in Kapitel 2.1 gesehen haben, liefert ein Schätzer bei jeder neuen Stichprobe einen
anderen Schätzwert. Wann ist ein Schätzer **gut**?

Ein anschauliches Bild: Stell dir einen Bogenschützen vor, der auf eine Zielscheibe schießt.
Das Zentrum der Scheibe ist der wahre Parameter $\theta$. Jeder Pfeil ist ein Schätzwert aus
einer zufälligen Stichprobe.

```{python}
#| echo: false
#| eval: true
np.random.seed(42)

def zielscheibe(ax, mu, sigma, title, n=80):
    pts = np.random.normal(mu, sigma, (n, 2))
    # Ringe von außen nach innen zeichnen, sonst verdeckt der größte Kreis alle anderen
    for r, c in [(4,'#adb5bd'),(3,'#ced4da'),(2,'#dee2e6'),(1,'#e9ecef')]:
        circle = plt.Circle((0, 0), r, color=c, fill=True)
        ax.add_patch(circle)
    ax.set_xlim(-5, 5); ax.set_ylim(-5, 5)
    ax.set_aspect('equal')
    ax.plot(*([0],[0]), 'k+', ms=12, mew=2.5, zorder=5)
    ax.scatter(pts[:,0], pts[:,1], s=20, color='#ae2012', alpha=0.7, zorder=4)
    # Schwerpunkt
    ax.plot(pts[:,0].mean(), pts[:,1].mean(), 'o', ms=10, color='#0a9396',
            markeredgecolor='white', markeredgewidth=1.5, zorder=6,
            label=f'Mittelpunkt der Treffer')
    ax.set_xticks([]); ax.set_yticks([])
    ax.set_title(title, fontsize=10)

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 4, figsize=(12, 3.5))

zielscheibe(axes[0], mu=[0,0], sigma=0.8, title='Erwartungstreu\n& effizient')
zielscheibe(axes[1], mu=[0,0], sigma=2.0, title='Erwartungstreu\n& ineffizient')
zielscheibe(axes[2], mu=[2,1], sigma=0.8, title='Nicht erw.treu\n& effizient (Bias!)')
zielscheibe(axes[3], mu=[2,1], sigma=2.0, title='Nicht erw.treu\n& ineffizient')

for ax in axes:
    ax.legend(loc='lower right', fontsize=7, framealpha=0.8)

plt.suptitle('Vier Schätzer im Vergleich: Zielscheiben-Metapher', y=1.02, fontsize=11)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

Wir wollen einen Schätzer, der:

1. **Im Mittel** den wahren Wert trifft (Erwartungstreue),
2. Wenig **streut** (Effizienz),
3. Für mehr Daten immer **genauer** wird (Konsistenz).

## Erwartungstreue (Bias)

::: {.callout-note icon=false}
## Definition: Bias und Erwartungstreue

Der **Bias** (oder die Verzerrung) eines Schätzers $\hat{\theta}$ für den Parameter $\theta$ ist
der systematische Schätzfehler:
$$\text{Bias}(\hat{\theta}) = E(\hat{\theta}) - \theta$$

Ein Schätzer heißt **erwartungstreu** (oder **unverzerrt**), wenn sein Bias null ist:
$$E(\hat{\theta}) = \theta$$
:::

### Das Stichprobenmittel ist erwartungstreu

Für unabhängige, identisch verteilte Werte mit Erwartungswert $\mu$ gilt:
$$E(\overline{X}_n) = E\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i\right) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n E(X_i) = \mu$$

Das Stichprobenmittel ist ein **erwartungstreuer** Schätzer für $\mu$.

### Warum $s^2$ den Faktor $1/(n-1)$ braucht

Wenn man die Varianz mit $1/n$ berechnet:
$$\tilde{s}^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2$$

dann gilt $E(\tilde{s}^2) = \frac{n-1}{n} \sigma^2 < \sigma^2$ – der Schätzer **unterschätzt**
systematisch die wahre Varianz $\sigma^2$. Mit dem Faktor $1/(n-1)$ hingegen gilt:
$$E(s^2) = \sigma^2 \quad \text{(erwartungstreu!)}$$

::: {.callout-caution}
## Häufiger Fehler: numpy vs. pandas Varianz

In Python berechnen `numpy` und `pandas` die Varianz unterschiedlich:

```python
import numpy as np, pandas as pd

x = [2, 4, 6, 8]
np.var(x)       # 1/n → NICHT erwartungstreu (= 5.0)
np.var(x, ddof=1)  # 1/(n-1) → erwartungstreu (= 6.667)
pd.Series(x).var()  # 1/(n-1) → erwartungstreu (= 6.667)
```

Für Schätzung der **Populations**varianz immer `ddof=1` verwenden!
:::

### Simulation: Bias sichtbar machen

```{python}
#| echo: false
#| eval: true
np.random.seed(1)
sigma2_true = 4.0
N_sims = 2000
ns = [5, 10, 20, 50, 100]

biased_means = []
unbiased_means = []

for n in ns:
    # beide Schätzer auf dieselben Stichproben anwenden
    samples = np.random.normal(0, 2, (N_sims, n))
    biased_means.append(np.var(samples, axis=1).mean())
    unbiased_means.append(np.var(samples, axis=1, ddof=1).mean())

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 4))
ax.plot(ns, biased_means, 'o-', color='#ae2012', lw=2, label='$\\tilde{s}^2$ (mit $1/n$) – verzerrt')
ax.plot(ns, unbiased_means, 's-', color='#0a9396', lw=2, label='$s^2$ (mit $1/(n-1)$) – erwartungstreu')
ax.axhline(sigma2_true, color='k', ls='--', lw=1.5, label=f'Wahrer Wert $\\sigma^2 = {sigma2_true}$')
ax.set_xlabel('Stichprobenumfang $n$')
ax.set_ylabel('Mittlerer Schätzwert (aus 2000 Stichproben)')
ax.set_title('Bias-Vergleich der beiden Varianz-Schätzer')
ax.legend(fontsize=9)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

Bei kleinen Stichproben ($n = 5$) schätzt $\tilde{s}^2$ die Varianz deutlich zu klein.
Der Unterschied wird mit wachsendem $n$ kleiner.

## Effizienz und Standardfehler

Ein Schätzer ist **effizient**, wenn er wenig streut. Das zentrale Maß dafür ist der
**Standardfehler**.

::: {.callout-note icon=false}
## Definition: Standardfehler

Der **Standardfehler** eines Schätzers $\hat{\theta}$ ist seine Standardabweichung:
$$\text{SE}(\hat{\theta}) = \sqrt{\text{Var}(\hat{\theta})}$$

Er misst, wie stark die Schätzwerte zwischen verschiedenen Stichproben streuen.
:::

### Standardfehler des Stichprobenmittels

Für unabhängige, identisch verteilte Werte mit Varianz $\sigma^2$ gilt:
$$\text{SE}(\overline{X}_n) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$$

::: {.callout-rechnen appearance="minimal"}
**Herleitung:**
$$\text{Var}(\overline{X}_n) = \text{Var}\!\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i\right)
= \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^n \text{Var}(X_i) = \frac{n \cdot \sigma^2}{n^2} = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}$$
:::

```{python}
#| echo: false
#| eval: true
np.random.seed(3)
true_mu = 3700
true_sigma = 460

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(11, 3.8))
ns_plot = [10, 30, 100]
colors = ['#ca6702', '#0a9396', '#005f73']

for ax, n, col in zip(axes, ns_plot, colors):
    estimates = [np.mean(np.random.normal(true_mu, true_sigma, n)) for _ in range(500)]
    ax.hist(estimates, bins=25, color=col, edgecolor='white', alpha=0.8, density=True)
    se = true_sigma / np.sqrt(n)
    xr = np.linspace(true_mu - 4*se, true_mu + 4*se, 300)
    ax.plot(xr, stats.norm.pdf(xr, true_mu, se), 'k', lw=2)
    ax.axvline(true_mu, color='#ae2012', lw=2, ls='--')
    ax.set_title(f'n = {n}\nSE = σ/√n = {se:.1f} g', fontsize=10)
    ax.set_xlabel('x̄ (g)')
    ax.set_xlim(2800, 4600)
    if ax == axes[0]:
        ax.set_ylabel('Dichte')

plt.suptitle('Verteilung von $\\bar{X}_n$ für verschiedene Stichprobengrößen', y=1.02, fontsize=11)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

**Fazit:** Mit $k$-mal so vielen Daten wird der Standardfehler nicht $k$-mal kleiner, sondern nur
um den Faktor $\sqrt{k}$. Um die Genauigkeit zu verdoppeln, braucht man die **vierfache** Datenmenge!

### Standardfehler in Python schätzen

Da $\sigma$ in der Praxis unbekannt ist, schätzt man den Standardfehler durch:
$$\widehat{\text{SE}}(\overline{X}_n) = \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}$$

```{pyodide-python}
import pandas as pd
import numpy as np
from pyodide.http import open_url

penguins = pd.read_csv(open_url("https://raw.githubusercontent.com/allisonhorst/palmerpenguins/main/inst/extdata/penguins.csv"))
masse = penguins[penguins['species'] == 'Adelie']['body_mass_g'].dropna()

n = len(masse)
s = masse.std()           # Stichproben-Standardabweichung (mit n-1)
se = s / np.sqrt(n)       # geschätzter Standardfehler

print(f"n = {n}")
print(f"s = {s:.1f} g")
print(f"Geschätzter Standardfehler SE = s/√n = {se:.1f} g")
```

## Konsistenz

::: {.callout-note icon=false}
## Definition: Konsistenz

Eine Folge von Schätzern $(\hat{\theta}_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ heißt **konsistent**, wenn die
Schätzwerte für wachsenden Stichprobenumfang $n$ gegen den wahren Parameterwert konvergieren:

$$\hat{\theta}_n \xrightarrow{n \to \infty} \theta \quad \text{(stochastisch)}$$

Anschaulich: Bei genug Daten wird die Schätzung beliebig genau.
:::

Das Stichprobenmittel ist konsistent, denn $\text{SE}(\overline{X}_n) = \sigma/\sqrt{n} \to 0$
für $n \to \infty$. Das ist eine Folgerung aus dem **Gesetz der großen Zahlen**.

### Simulation: Konvergenz des Stichprobenmittels


Das Stichprobenmittel pendelt zunächst stark, nähert sich dann aber immer enger dem wahren

## Der MSE: Bias und Varianz zusammen

Der **mittlere quadratische Fehler** (MSE) vereint Bias und Varianz in einer Größe:

$$\text{MSE}(\hat{\theta}) = \text{Var}(\hat{\theta}) + \text{Bias}(\hat{\theta})^2$$

Das nennt sich die **Bias-Varianz-Zerlegung**. Ein guter Schätzer hat sowohl kleinen Bias als
auch kleine Varianz – beide Terme tragen zum Gesamtfehler bei.

```{python}
#| echo: false
#| eval: true
ns = np.arange(5, 101)

# Verzerrter (1/n) vs. erwartungstreuer (1/(n-1)) Schätzer für σ² = 4, Normalverteilung
sigma2 = 4.0

# Bias²: E(~s²) = (n-1)/n * σ², also Bias = -σ²/n
bias_sq = (sigma2 / ns)**2
var_biased = 2 * (ns - 1) * sigma2**2 / ns**2  # Var(~s²) = 2(n-1)σ⁴/n²
var_unbiased = 2 * sigma2**2 / (ns - 1)        # Var(s²)  = 2σ⁴/(n-1)

mse_biased = bias_sq + var_biased
mse_unbiased = var_unbiased  # bias=0

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 3.8))

ax = axes[0]
ax.plot(ns, bias_sq, '#ae2012', lw=2, label='Bias²')
ax.plot(ns, var_biased, '#0a9396', lw=2, label='Varianz')
ax.plot(ns, mse_biased, 'k', lw=2, ls='--', label='MSE = Bias² + Varianz')
ax.set_title('Verzerrt: $\\tilde{s}^2$ (mit $1/n$)', fontsize=10)
ax.set_xlabel('n'); ax.set_ylabel('Fehler'); ax.legend(fontsize=8); ax.set_xlim(5, 100); ax.set_ylim(0, 8.5)

ax2 = axes[1]
ax2.plot(ns, np.zeros_like(ns), '#ae2012', lw=2, label='Bias² = 0')
ax2.plot(ns, var_unbiased, '#0a9396', lw=2, label='Varianz = MSE')
ax2.plot(ns, mse_biased, color='#999', lw=1.5, ls='--', label='MSE von $\\tilde{s}^2$ (zum Vergleich)')
ax2.set_title('Erwartungstreu: $s^2$ (mit $1/(n-1)$)', fontsize=10)
ax2.set_xlabel('n'); ax2.legend(fontsize=8); ax2.set_xlim(5, 100); ax2.set_ylim(0, 8.5)

plt.suptitle('MSE-Zerlegung der beiden Varianz-Schätzer ($\\sigma^2 = 4$, Normalverteilung)', fontsize=11, y=1.02)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

Überraschend: $\tilde{s}^2$ hat zwar einen Bias, streut aber weniger als $s^2$. Bei normalverteilten Daten ist sein MSE für **jedes** $n$ kleiner (gestrichelte Linie rechts). Erwartungstreue ist also nicht automatisch das Wichtigste. Man verwendet $s^2$ trotzdem meist, weil es im Mittel richtig liegt und weil die Formeln für Konfidenzintervalle und Tests (Kapitel 2.4 und 2.5) darauf aufbauen.

---

## Zusammenfassung

| Kriterium | Was wird bewertet? | Formel / Bedingung |
|-----------|--------------------|--------------------|
| **Erwartungstreue** | Systematischer Fehler | $E(\hat{\theta}) = \theta$ (Bias = 0) |
| **Standardfehler** | Streuung der Schätzwerte | $\text{SE} = \sqrt{\text{Var}(\hat{\theta})}$ |
| **Konsistenz** | Verhalten für $n \to \infty$ | $\hat{\theta}_n \to \theta$ |
| **MSE** | Gesamtfehler | $\text{MSE} = \text{Var} + \text{Bias}^2$ |

Für die Praxis gilt: Die Standardschätzer $\overline{X}_n$ (für $\mu$) und $s^2$ (für $\sigma^2$)
sind erwartungstreu und konsistent. Der Standardfehler des Mittelwerts beträgt $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$.

## Introduction: what makes a good estimator? {#einführung-was-macht-einen-guten-schätzer-aus}

As we saw in section 2.1, an estimator gives a different estimate for every new sample. When is
an estimator **good**?

A vivid picture: imagine an archer shooting at a target. The center of the target is the true
parameter $\theta$. Every arrow is an estimate from a random sample.

```{python}
#| echo: false
#| eval: true
np.random.seed(42)

def target(ax, mu, sigma, title, n=80):
    pts = np.random.normal(mu, sigma, (n, 2))
    # draw rings from the outside in, otherwise the largest circle hides all others
    for r, c in [(4,'#adb5bd'),(3,'#ced4da'),(2,'#dee2e6'),(1,'#e9ecef')]:
        circle = plt.Circle((0, 0), r, color=c, fill=True)
        ax.add_patch(circle)
    ax.set_xlim(-5, 5); ax.set_ylim(-5, 5)
    ax.set_aspect('equal')
    ax.plot(*([0],[0]), 'k+', ms=12, mew=2.5, zorder=5)
    ax.scatter(pts[:,0], pts[:,1], s=20, color='#ae2012', alpha=0.7, zorder=4)
    # center of the hits
    ax.plot(pts[:,0].mean(), pts[:,1].mean(), 'o', ms=10, color='#0a9396',
            markeredgecolor='white', markeredgewidth=1.5, zorder=6,
            label=f'Center of the hits')
    ax.set_xticks([]); ax.set_yticks([])
    ax.set_title(title, fontsize=10)

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 4, figsize=(12, 3.5))

target(axes[0], mu=[0,0], sigma=0.8, title='Unbiased\n& efficient')
target(axes[1], mu=[0,0], sigma=2.0, title='Unbiased\n& inefficient')
target(axes[2], mu=[2,1], sigma=0.8, title='Biased\n& efficient (bias!)')
target(axes[3], mu=[2,1], sigma=2.0, title='Biased\n& inefficient')

for ax in axes:
    ax.legend(loc='lower right', fontsize=7, framealpha=0.8)

plt.show()
```

We want an estimator that:

1. hits the true value **on average** (unbiasedness),
2. **spreads** little (efficiency),
3. becomes ever more **precise** with more data (consistency).

## Unbiasedness (bias) {#erwartungstreue-bias}

::: {.callout-note icon=false}
## Definition: bias and unbiasedness

The **bias** of an estimator $\hat{\theta}$ for the parameter $\theta$ is the systematic
estimation error:
$$\text{Bias}(\hat{\theta}) = E(\hat{\theta}) - \theta$$

An estimator is called **unbiased** if its bias is zero:
$$E(\hat{\theta}) = \theta$$
:::

### The sample mean is unbiased {#das-stichprobenmittel-ist-erwartungstreu}

For independent, identically distributed values with expected value $\mu$:
$$E(\overline{X}_n) = E\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i\right) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n E(X_i) = \mu$$

The sample mean is an **unbiased** estimator for $\mu$.

### Why $s^2$ needs the factor $1/(n-1)$ {#warum-s2-den-faktor-1n-1-braucht}

If you compute the variance with $1/n$:
$$\tilde{s}^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2$$

then $E(\tilde{s}^2) = \frac{n-1}{n} \sigma^2 < \sigma^2$ – the estimator systematically
**underestimates** the true variance $\sigma^2$. With the factor $1/(n-1)$, on the other hand:
$$E(s^2) = \sigma^2 \quad \text{(unbiased!)}$$

::: {.callout-caution}
## Common mistake: numpy vs. pandas variance

In Python, `numpy` and `pandas` compute the variance differently:

```python
import numpy as np, pandas as pd

x = [2, 4, 6, 8]
np.var(x)       # 1/n → NOT unbiased (= 5.0)
np.var(x, ddof=1)  # 1/(n-1) → unbiased (= 6.667)
pd.Series(x).var()  # 1/(n-1) → unbiased (= 6.667)
```

To estimate the **population** variance, always use `ddof=1`!
:::

### Simulation: making the bias visible {#simulation-bias-sichtbar-machen}

```{python}
#| echo: false
#| eval: true
np.random.seed(1)
sigma2_true = 4.0
N_sims = 2000
ns = [5, 10, 20, 50, 100]

biased_means = []
unbiased_means = []

for n in ns:
    # apply both estimators to the same samples
    samples = np.random.normal(0, 2, (N_sims, n))
    biased_means.append(np.var(samples, axis=1).mean())
    unbiased_means.append(np.var(samples, axis=1, ddof=1).mean())

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 4))
ax.plot(ns, biased_means, 'o-', color='#ae2012', lw=2, label='$\\tilde{s}^2$ (with $1/n$) – biased')
ax.plot(ns, unbiased_means, 's-', color='#0a9396', lw=2, label='$s^2$ (with $1/(n-1)$) – unbiased')
ax.axhline(sigma2_true, color='k', ls='--', lw=1.5, label=f'True value $\\sigma^2 = {sigma2_true}$')
ax.set_xlabel('Sample size $n$')
ax.set_ylabel('Mean estimate (from 2000 samples)')
ax.set_title('Bias comparison of the two variance estimators')
ax.legend(fontsize=9)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

For small samples ($n = 5$), $\tilde{s}^2$ clearly underestimates the variance. The difference
becomes smaller as $n$ grows.

## Efficiency and standard error {#effizienz-und-standardfehler}

An estimator is **efficient** if it spreads little. The central measure for this is the
**standard error**.

::: {.callout-note icon=false}
## Definition: standard error

The **standard error** of an estimator $\hat{\theta}$ is its standard deviation:
$$\text{SE}(\hat{\theta}) = \sqrt{\text{Var}(\hat{\theta})}$$

It measures how much the estimates vary between different samples.
:::

### Standard error of the sample mean {#standardfehler-des-stichprobenmittels}

For independent, identically distributed values with variance $\sigma^2$:
$$\text{SE}(\overline{X}_n) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$$

::: {.callout-rechnen appearance="minimal"}
**Derivation:**
$$\text{Var}(\overline{X}_n) = \text{Var}\!\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i\right)
= \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^n \text{Var}(X_i) = \frac{n \cdot \sigma^2}{n^2} = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}$$
:::

```{python}
#| echo: false
#| eval: true
np.random.seed(3)
true_mu = 3700
true_sigma = 460

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(11, 3.8))
ns_plot = [10, 30, 100]
colors = ['#ca6702', '#0a9396', '#005f73']

for ax, n, col in zip(axes, ns_plot, colors):
    estimates = [np.mean(np.random.normal(true_mu, true_sigma, n)) for _ in range(500)]
    ax.hist(estimates, bins=25, color=col, edgecolor='white', alpha=0.8, density=True)
    se = true_sigma / np.sqrt(n)
    xr = np.linspace(true_mu - 4*se, true_mu + 4*se, 300)
    ax.plot(xr, stats.norm.pdf(xr, true_mu, se), 'k', lw=2)
    ax.axvline(true_mu, color='#ae2012', lw=2, ls='--')
    ax.set_title(f'n = {n}\nSE = σ/√n = {se:.1f} g', fontsize=10)
    ax.set_xlabel('x̄ (g)')
    ax.set_xlim(2800, 4600)
    if ax == axes[0]:
        ax.set_ylabel('Density')

plt.suptitle('Distribution of $\\bar{X}_n$ for different sample sizes', y=1.02, fontsize=11)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

**Conclusion:** With $k$ times as much data, the standard error does not become $k$ times smaller,
but only smaller by the factor $\sqrt{k}$. To double the precision you need **four times** as
much data!

### Estimating the standard error in Python {#standardfehler-in-python-schätzen}

Since $\sigma$ is unknown in practice, the standard error is estimated by:
$$\widehat{\text{SE}}(\overline{X}_n) = \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}$$

```{pyodide-python}
import pandas as pd
import numpy as np
from pyodide.http import open_url

penguins = pd.read_csv(open_url("https://raw.githubusercontent.com/allisonhorst/palmerpenguins/main/inst/extdata/penguins.csv"))

n = len(mass)
s = mass.std()            # sample standard deviation (with n-1)
se = s / np.sqrt(n)       # estimated standard error

print(f"n = {n}")
print(f"s = {s:.1f} g")
print(f"Estimated standard error SE = s/√n = {se:.1f} g")
```

$n = 25$ animals? How many animals do you need for it to be only half as large? And: for
$\sigma^2 = 4$ and $n = 5$, what expected value and what bias does $\tilde{s}^2$ have?

## Consistency {#konsistenz}

::: {.callout-note icon=false}
## Definition: consistency

A sequence of estimators $(\hat{\theta}_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ is called **consistent** if the
estimates converge to the true parameter value as the sample size $n$ grows:

$$\hat{\theta}_n \xrightarrow{n \to \infty} \theta \quad \text{(in probability)}$$

Intuitively: with enough data the estimate becomes arbitrarily precise.
:::

The sample mean is consistent, because $\text{SE}(\overline{X}_n) = \sigma/\sqrt{n} \to 0$ for
$n \to \infty$. This is a consequence of the **law of large numbers**.

### Simulation: convergence of the sample mean {#simulation-konvergenz-des-stichprobenmittels}

```{python}
#| echo: false
#| eval: true
np.random.seed(5)
true_mu = 3700.0
true_sigma = 460.0

max_n = 300
running_means = np.cumsum(np.random.normal(true_mu, true_sigma, max_n)) / np.arange(1, max_n + 1)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(9, 4))
ns = np.arange(1, max_n + 1)
ax.plot(ns, running_means, '#0a9396', lw=1.8, label='running mean $\\bar{x}_n$')
ax.axhline(true_mu, color='#ae2012', lw=2, ls='--', label=f'True value μ = {true_mu:.0f} g')

se = true_sigma / np.sqrt(ns)
ax.fill_between(ns, true_mu - 2*se, true_mu + 2*se, alpha=0.15, color='#ae2012',
                label='±2·SE band')

ax.set_xlabel('Sample size n')
ax.set_ylabel('x̄ (g)')
ax.set_title('Consistency: $\\bar{X}_n$ converges to μ for $n \\to \\infty$')
ax.legend(fontsize=9)
ax.set_xlim(1, max_n)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

The sample mean fluctuates strongly at first, but then gets ever closer to the true value. The
±2·SE band shows how the uncertainty decreases with $1/\sqrt{n}$.

## MSE: bias and variance together {#der-mse-bias-und-varianz-zusammen}

The **mean squared error** (MSE) combines bias and variance in one quantity:

$$\text{MSE}(\hat{\theta}) = \text{Var}(\hat{\theta}) + \text{Bias}(\hat{\theta})^2$$

This is called the **bias-variance decomposition**. A good estimator has both small bias and
small variance – both terms contribute to the total error.

```{python}
#| echo: false
#| eval: true
ns = np.arange(5, 101)

# Biased (1/n) vs. unbiased (1/(n-1)) estimator for σ² = 4, normal distribution
sigma2 = 4.0

# Bias²: E(~s²) = (n-1)/n * σ², so Bias = -σ²/n
bias_sq = (sigma2 / ns)**2
var_biased = 2 * (ns - 1) * sigma2**2 / ns**2  # Var(~s²) = 2(n-1)σ⁴/n²
var_unbiased = 2 * sigma2**2 / (ns - 1)        # Var(s²)  = 2σ⁴/(n-1)

mse_biased = bias_sq + var_biased
mse_unbiased = var_unbiased  # bias=0

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 3.8))

ax = axes[0]
ax.plot(ns, bias_sq, '#ae2012', lw=2, label='Bias²')
ax.plot(ns, var_biased, '#0a9396', lw=2, label='Variance')
ax.plot(ns, mse_biased, 'k', lw=2, ls='--', label='MSE = Bias² + Variance')
ax.set_title('Biased: $\\tilde{s}^2$ (with $1/n$)', fontsize=10)
ax.set_xlabel('n'); ax.set_ylabel('Error'); ax.legend(fontsize=8); ax.set_xlim(5, 100); ax.set_ylim(0, 8.5)

ax2 = axes[1]
ax2.plot(ns, np.zeros_like(ns), '#ae2012', lw=2, label='Bias² = 0')
ax2.plot(ns, var_unbiased, '#0a9396', lw=2, label='Variance = MSE')
ax2.plot(ns, mse_biased, color='#999', lw=1.5, ls='--', label='MSE of $\\tilde{s}^2$ (for comparison)')
ax2.set_title('Unbiased: $s^2$ (with $1/(n-1)$)', fontsize=10)
ax2.set_xlabel('n'); ax2.legend(fontsize=8); ax2.set_xlim(5, 100); ax2.set_ylim(0, 8.5)

plt.suptitle('MSE decomposition of the two variance estimators ($\\sigma^2 = 4$, normal distribution)', fontsize=11, y=1.02)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

Surprisingly, $\tilde{s}^2$ is biased but spreads less than $s^2$. For normally distributed data
its MSE is smaller for **every** $n$ (dashed line on the right). So unbiasedness is not
automatically the most important thing. $s^2$ is still usually used, because it is right on
average and because the formulas for confidence intervals and tests (sections 2.4 and 2.5) build
on it.

## Summary {#zusammenfassung}

| Criterion | What is assessed? | Formula / condition |
|-----------|--------------------|--------------------|
| **Unbiasedness** | Systematic error | $E(\hat{\theta}) = \theta$ (Bias = 0) |
| **Standard error** | Spread of the estimates | $\text{SE} = \sqrt{\text{Var}(\hat{\theta})}$ |
| **Consistency** | Behavior for $n \to \infty$ | $\hat{\theta}_n \to \theta$ |
| **MSE** | Total error | $\text{MSE} = \text{Var} + \text{Bias}^2$ |

In practice: the standard estimators $\overline{X}_n$ (for $\mu$) and $s^2$ (for $\sigma^2$) are
unbiased and consistent. The standard error of the mean is $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$.
