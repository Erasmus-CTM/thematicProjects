Finding Asymptotes

Your answers are saved only in this browser.

What is an asymptote?

Before we get to the Python implementation, it is important to know what an asymptote is in general. An asymptote is a curve that the function approaches more and more closely at infinity.

Important: This does not mean that the function and the curve (usually a straight line) never meet. There are different possibilities.

  1. They never meet, e.g.: \(f(x)=\frac{1}{x}\)
  2. They meet finitely often before they no longer meet at infinity, e.g.: \(f(x) = \frac{x^3 + 2x^2 + 1}{x^2 + 1}\)
  3. They meet infinitely often because the function oscillates around the asymptote (i.e. in the form of a wave), crossing it once with every oscillation, e.g.: \(f(x) = \frac{\sin(x)}{x}\).

    • Vertical asymptotes
    • Horizontal asymptotes
    • Oblique asymptotes
  • Non-linear asymptotes

Vertical asymptotes

A vertical asymptote is a vertical line of the form \(x = a\) that the function approaches. Such asymptotes typically occur where the function has gaps in its domain, e.g. with division by zero.
They can also occur with other functions such as logarithms, but for simplicity we only consider cases with division by zero. Advanced learners are welcome to add a check for logarithmic functions.

The graph of the function \(f(x)\) has a vertical asymptote at \(x=a\) if at least one of the following limits holds: \[ \lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = \pm\infty \quad \text{or} \quad \lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) = \pm\infty \]

Horizontal asymptotes

A horizontal asymptote is a horizontal line of the form \(y=a\) that the function approaches in its end behavior.

The line \(y=a\) is a horizontal asymptote of \(f(x)\) if for \(a\in \mathbb{R}\): \[ \lim_{x \to \infty} f(x) = a \quad \text{or} \quad \lim_{x \to -\infty} f(x) = a \]

Oblique asymptotes

An oblique (or slant) asymptote is a line of the form \(y = mx + b\) that the function approaches.
It occurs with rational functions when the degree of the numerator polynomial is exactly one greater than the degree of the denominator polynomial and the polynomial division leaves a remainder. The result of the polynomial division is the equation of the asymptote.
The line \(f_a(x) = mx + b\) is an oblique asymptote of \(f\) if the distance between \(f\) and \(f_a\) tends to 0 at infinity: \[ \lim_{x \to \infty} | f(x) - f_a(x) | = 0 \quad \text{or} \quad \lim_{x \to -\infty} | f(x) - f_a(x) | = 0 \]

Non-linear asymptotes

This section is an optional bonus and has no automatic check.

At school, usually only straight-line asymptotes are covered. However, there are also non-linear asymptotes. This curve that the function approaches at infinity is also called an approximating curve or curvilinear asymptote.

Like oblique asymptotes, such an asymptote arises from a rational function, except that in this case the degree of the numerator polynomial is more than one greater than the degree of the denominator polynomial \((\text{numerator degree} > \text{denominator degree} + 1)\).
As with oblique asymptotes, the existence of a remainder in the polynomial division is important here. If there is none, the function does not approach the approximating curve at infinity but is identical to it everywhere, so it is not an asymptote!
Here, too, you find the approximating curve by polynomial division.

# Was ist eine Asymptote?

Bevor es um die Python-Implementierung geht, ist es wichtig zu wissen, was eine Asymptote im Allgemeinen ist.
Eine **Asymptote ist** eine Kurve, der sich die Funktion **im Unendlichen** immer weiter annähert.  

**Wichtig**: Das heißt nicht, dass sich die Funktion und die Kurve (meistens handelt es sich um eine Gerade) nie treffen. Es gibt hier unterschiedliche Möglichkeiten.

1.  Sie treffen sich nie, z.B.: $f(x)=\frac{1}{x}$
2.  Sie treffen sich endlich oft, bevor sie sich im Unendlichen nicht mehr treffen, z.B.: $f(x) = \frac{x^3 + 2x^2 + 1}{x^2 + 1}$
3.  Sie treffen sich unendlich oft, da die Funktion um die Asymptote oszilliert (d.h. in Form einer Schwingung), also diese auch unendlich oft nicht schneidet, aber bei jeder Schwingung einmal schneidet, z.B.: $f(x) = \frac{\sin(x)}{x}$.

# Lineare Asymptoten

## Vertikale Asymptoten

Eine **vertikale Asymptote** ist eine senkrechte Gerade der Form $x = a$, an die sich die Funktion annähert. Solche Asymptoten treten typischerweise an Stellen auf, an denen die Funktion Definitionslücken hat, z.B. bei **[Division durch Null](../Kapitel_1/definitionsbereich.qmd#gebrochen-rationale-funktionen)**.  
_Es kann jedoch auch bei anderen Funktionen wie Logarithmen auftreten der Einfachheit werden jedoch nur Fälle mit Division durch Null betrachtet. Fortgeschrittene können eine Abfrage für eine Logarithmusfunktion gerne mit einbauen._

**Der Graph der Funktion $f(x)$ hat bei $x=a$ eine vertikale Asymptote, wenn mindestens einer der folgenden Grenzwerte gilt:**
$$ \lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = \pm\infty \quad \text{oder} \quad \lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) = \pm\infty $$

## Schiefe Asymptoten

Eine **schiefe (oder schräge) Asymptote** ist eine Gerade der Form $y = mx + b$, an die sich die Funktion annähert.  
Sie tritt bei gebrochen-rationalen Funktionen auf, wenn der Grad des Zählerpolynoms genau um eins größer ist als der Grad des Nennerpolynoms und bei der Polynomdivision ein Rest existiert.
Das Ergebnis der Polynomdivision ist die Geradengleichung der Asymptote.  
**Die Gerade $f_a(x) = mx + b$ ist eine *schiefe* Asymptote von $f$, wenn der Abstand zwischen $f$ und $f_a$ im Unendlichen gegen 0 geht:** 
$$ \lim_{x \to \infty} | f(x) - f_a(x) | = 0 \quad \text{oder} \quad \lim_{x \to -\infty} | f(x) - f_a(x) | = 0 $$

# Nicht-lineare Asymptoten 

*Dieser Abschnitt ist ein freiwilliger **Bonus** und hat keine automatische Prüfung.*

_In der Schule wird sich meist nur mit geraden Asymptoten beschäftigt. Es gibt jedoch auch nicht-lineare oder nicht-gerade Asymptoten. Diese **Kurve** an die sich die Funktion **im Unendlichen** annähert wird auch **Näherungskurve** oder **kurvenförmige Asymptote** genannt._

Eine solche Asymptote entsteht ähnlich wie schiefe Asymptoten durch eine gebrochen-rationale Funktion, nur dass in diesem Fall der Grad des Zählerpolynoms **mehr als eins größer** ist als der Grad des Nennerpolynoms $(Zählergrad > Nennergrad + 1)$.  
Ähnlich wie bei den schiefen Asymptoten ist es auch hier die **Existenz eines Rests bei der Polynomdivision wichtig**. Wenn sie keinen hat, nähert sich die Funktion der **Näherungskurve** im Unendlichen nicht an, sondern ist durchgehend mit ihr identisch, wodurch es sich nicht um eine Asymptote handelt!  
Auch hier findet man die Näherungskurve durch **Polynomdivision**.

```{pyodide-python}
plt.plot(x_werte, x_werte**2, "--", label="Näherungskurve y = x²")
plt.axvline(0, color="gray", linestyle=":", label="vertikale Asymptote x = 0")
plt.ylim(-10, 30)
plt.legend()
plt.grid(True)
plt.show()
```

# What is an asymptote?

Before we get to the Python implementation, it is important to know what an asymptote is in general.
An **asymptote is** a curve that the function approaches more and more closely **at infinity**.  

**Important**: This does not mean that the function and the curve (usually a straight line) never meet. There are different possibilities.

1.  They never meet, e.g.: $f(x)=\frac{1}{x}$
2.  They meet finitely often before they no longer meet at infinity, e.g.: $f(x) = \frac{x^3 + 2x^2 + 1}{x^2 + 1}$
3.  They meet infinitely often because the function oscillates around the asymptote (i.e. in the form of a wave), crossing it once with every oscillation, e.g.: $f(x) = \frac{\sin(x)}{x}$.

* **[Linear asymptotes:](#linear-asymptotes)**  
  *   **[Vertical asymptotes](#vertical-asymptotes)**
  *   **[Horizontal asymptotes](#horizontal-asymptotes)**
  *   **[Oblique asymptotes](#oblique-asymptotes)**
* **[Non-linear asymptotes](#non-linear-asymptotes)**

# Linear asymptotes

## Vertical asymptotes

A **vertical asymptote** is a vertical line of the form $x = a$ that the function approaches. Such asymptotes typically occur where the function has gaps in its domain, e.g. with **[division by zero](../Kapitel_1/definitionsbereich.qmd#rational-functions)**.  
_They can also occur with other functions such as logarithms, but for simplicity we only consider cases with division by zero. Advanced learners are welcome to add a check for logarithmic functions._

**The graph of the function $f(x)$ has a vertical asymptote at $x=a$ if at least one of the following limits holds:**
$$ \lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = \pm\infty \quad \text{or} \quad \lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) = \pm\infty $$

## Horizontal asymptotes

A **horizontal asymptote** is a horizontal line of the form $y=a$ that the function approaches in its **[end behavior](globalverhalten.qmd)**.

**The line $y=a$ is a horizontal asymptote of $f(x)$ if for $a\in \mathbb{R}$:** 
$$ \lim_{x \to \infty} f(x) = a \quad \text{or} \quad \lim_{x \to -\infty} f(x) = a $$


## Oblique asymptotes

An **oblique (or slant) asymptote** is a line of the form $y = mx + b$ that the function approaches.  
It occurs with rational functions when the degree of the numerator polynomial is exactly one greater than the degree of the denominator polynomial and the polynomial division leaves a remainder.
The result of the polynomial division is the equation of the asymptote.  
**The line $f_a(x) = mx + b$ is an *oblique* asymptote of $f$ if the distance between $f$ and $f_a$ tends to 0 at infinity:** 
$$ \lim_{x \to \infty} | f(x) - f_a(x) | = 0 \quad \text{or} \quad \lim_{x \to -\infty} | f(x) - f_a(x) | = 0 $$ 

**Quick check:**

```{math-exercise}

#| caption: Pre-check – compare degrees
By how much is the numerator degree greater than the denominator degree for $f(x)=\dfrac{x^3+1}{x^2-4}$? Difference $=$ _[1]
```

```{math-exercise}

#| caption: Pre-check – polynomial division
#| vars: x
Carry out the polynomial division $(x^2+x+2):(x-1)$ by hand. The result without remainder is $x+2$, the remainder is $4$. So which oblique asymptote does $f(x)=\dfrac{x^2+x+2}{x-1}$ have? $y =$ __[x+2]
```

# Non-linear asymptotes 

*This section is an optional **bonus** and has no automatic check.*

_At school, usually only straight-line asymptotes are covered. However, there are also non-linear asymptotes. This **curve** that the function approaches **at infinity** is also called an **approximating curve** or **curvilinear asymptote**._

Like oblique asymptotes, such an asymptote arises from a rational function, except that in this case the degree of the numerator polynomial is **more than one greater** than the degree of the denominator polynomial $(\text{numerator degree} > \text{denominator degree} + 1)$.  
As with oblique asymptotes, the **existence of a remainder in the polynomial division is important** here. If there is none, the function does not approach the **approximating curve** at infinity but is identical to it everywhere, so it is not an asymptote!  
Here, too, you find the approximating curve by **polynomial division**.

#### Python implementation and visualization

In principle, the idea for calculating a non-linear asymptote is _very_ similar to calculating an oblique asymptote.  
That is why this is an exercise:

```{pyodide-python}

#Insert your code here
```

The following plot shows what this looks like for $f(x)=\frac{x^3+1}{x}$: the polynomial division gives $x^2$ with remainder $1$, so the approximating curve is the parabola $y=x^2$. Run the block and **zoom in with the magnifier** on an area far to the right or left: the further out, the closer the function and the parabola lie. Near $x=0$ you can also see the vertical asymptote.

```{pyodide-python}

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

f_values = (x_values**3 + 1) / x_values
f_values[np.abs(x_values) < 0.05] = np.nan    # gap at x = 0 (gap in the domain), otherwise plt.plot connects both branches

plt.plot(x_values, f_values, label="f(x) = (x³+1)/x")
plt.plot(x_values, x_values**2, "--", label="approximating curve y = x²")
plt.axvline(0, color="gray", linestyle=":", label="vertical asymptote x = 0")
plt.ylim(-10, 30)
plt.legend()
plt.grid(True)
plt.show()
```
